
# Private Equity and Labour Adjustment across Pre-Deal Hiring States

## Abstract

Private equity research documents substantial heterogeneity in firms’ real-side outcomes, but existing evidence has largely organised this variation around the transaction or the sponsor. We examine whether the target’s own hiring condition before investment also distinguishes its subsequent labour adjustment. Using monthly Korean National Pension records linked to private equity investments, we construct a primary state-balanced matched sample of 286 events. Firms with lower pre-deal hiring intensity exhibit substantially larger post-investment increases in hiring: an interquartile movement toward lower prior hiring is associated with a 0.2005-log-point larger treated-minus-control response [0.0900, 0.3177]. The underlying state gradient is 0.7101 [0.3187, 1.1254] and lies well above the distribution generated by 1,246 untreated pseudo-events processed through the same procedure; the empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.0005. Balancing controls on the pre-deal hiring state is consequential because the same state predicts untreated hiring dynamics: the control-side slope with respect to the treated target’s state falls from 0.2950 under conventional matching to 0.0056 under state-balanced matching. The relationship remains in worker-entry counts that do not use employment as a denominator. Low-hiring targets also exhibit a larger churn response, while the employment gradient is negative and the separation gradient is too imprecise to establish a distinct exit margin. The evidence therefore locates the strongest heterogeneity in worker inflows rather than correspondingly larger net-employment gains. The design addresses observable state-dependent rebound but does not rule out forward-looking selection on sponsor private information.

**Keywords:** Private equity; Employment; Worker flows; Hiring; Matching

**JEL classification:** G24; G34; J23; J63

# 1. Introduction

Employment outcomes following private equity investment differ markedly across target firms. Existing research has organised much of this heterogeneity around characteristics of the transaction, the financing environment, and the private equity sponsor. Those dimensions are economically important, but they leave open a more basic target-side question: do firms entering private equity investment from different recent operating conditions subsequently adjust their workforces differently?

We study one such condition-the target’s recent hiring history. A firm that has hired few workers for a sustained period enters a transaction from a different position from an otherwise comparable firm that has been adding workers regularly. The distinction may reflect differences in demand, financing, managerial capacity, organisational adjustment, or simply the timing of recent labour demand. We do not observe which of these forces generates the state. Our question is narrower: whether recent pre-deal hiring intensity distinguishes the magnitude of the target’s subsequent worker-inflow response.

That question presents a counterfactual problem. Low-hiring firms may increase hiring even in the absence of private equity investment. If this untreated rebound varies with the same pre-deal state used to organise heterogeneous responses, conventional matching on industry, size, prior employment growth, and age need not provide a neutral comparison along that dimension. Controls assigned to low- and high-hiring targets can themselves follow systematically different subsequent paths. An estimated gradient in treated-minus-control outcomes can then combine heterogeneity among targets with heterogeneity in the constructed counterfactual.

We address the two sides of this problem separately. First, the primary matching design balances the pre-deal hiring state itself, in addition to conventional firm characteristics. Second, rather than compare the target gradient with a zero-centred null, we construct an empirical reference distribution from untreated pseudo-events processed through the same state definition, matching procedure, outcome transformation, adjustment, and estimation steps. This reference distribution allows low-hiring untreated firms to rebound and asks whether the corresponding gradient among private equity targets is unusual relative to that background state dependence.

The analysis links private equity investments in Korea to monthly administrative records from the National Pension Scheme. The records report insured employment together with entries and exits of insured workers. Their monthly frequency allows the state and outcome to be measured over separate periods: the hiring state uses months $-24$ through $-13$, the baseline outcome months $-12$ through $-1$, and the post-investment outcome months $+1$ through $+12$. We interpret pension entries as administrative insured-worker inflows. For convenience we refer to them as hires or hiring after this qualification, but the records do not independently identify the recruiting decision that generated each entry.

The baseline matched universe contains 379 private equity investment events. The primary heterogeneity analysis contains 286 events with a complete earlier state, complete outcome windows, and eligible state-balanced controls. Higher values of the state correspond to lower pre-deal hiring intensity. The primary outcome is the treated-minus-control change in the log hiring rate between the twelve months before and after investment.

The response varies sharply with the target’s pre-deal state. The continuous state gradient is 0.7101, with a 95 percent treated-event bootstrap interval of [0.3187, 1.1254]. An interquartile movement toward lower prior hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.2005-log-point larger matched response [0.0900, 0.3177], equivalent to approximately a 22 percent larger relative hiring-rate response. This quantity is a cross-target gradient in the matched post-investment response; it is not the average effect of private equity investment.

Untreated firms also display state-dependent rebound. The empirical reference distribution is constructed from 1,205 untreated pseudo-events and has a mean gradient of about 0.101. The target gradient exceeds this mean by about 0.609 log points and lies 3.96 reference-distribution standard deviations above it. The empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.0005. The main empirical result is therefore not the mechanical proposition that firms with low prior hiring subsequently hire more. It is that the state gradient among private equity targets is unusually large relative to the state-dependent hiring movements generated by untreated firms under the same empirical procedure.

How the counterfactual is constructed materially affects the estimate. On the 286 target events common to both designs, conventional matching produces a gradient of 0.4698, while state-balanced matching produces 0.7101. An unwinsorised decomposition clarifies the source of the difference. Because the treated events are held fixed in this comparison, the treated-side slope is 0.8425 under both procedures. The informative change is on the control side: the slope of the assigned control outcome with respect to the treated target’s state falls from 0.2950 under conventional matching to 0.0056 after balancing the state. In this setting, the moderator is therefore not merely a device for sorting treated responses. It is also relevant to the construction of the untreated path against which those responses are evaluated.

This feature of the design has a broader implication for empirical work on heterogeneous responses. A pre-treatment characteristic can be balanced on conventional observables yet still predict untreated outcome dynamics. When the same characteristic is used to organise response heterogeneity, a matched comparison can inherit a moderator-dependent counterfactual even if average covariate balance appears acceptable. The implication is not that every heterogeneity analysis must match directly on its moderator, but that researchers should examine whether the proposed moderator also organises untreated paths and construct the comparison accordingly when it does.

The hiring-state result is not generated primarily by employment in the denominator. Using the raw log number of worker entries yields a positive state gradient of 0.6315. A diagnostic specification that additionally includes the change in log employment gives a gradient of 0.6957. Because employment is itself a post-investment outcome, the latter specification is not interpreted as a causal adjustment. Together, the count specifications show that the central pattern is present in worker inflows themselves rather than arising solely from the scaling of flows by employment.

Net employment follows a different cross-target pattern. Employment rises relative to matched controls on average: the twelve-month average matched change in log employment is 0.0878 [0.0462, 0.1300]. Across pre-deal hiring states, however, the employment gradient is negative at $-0.0580$. Targets that enter the transaction with lower hiring intensity therefore exhibit larger worker-inflow responses without correspondingly larger net-employment gains along the same state dimension. Worker churn also has a positive state gradient, but worker entries are themselves a component of churn. The separation gradient is positive but imprecise. We therefore do not infer a distinct exit or replacement mechanism from these data.

The distinction between worker inflows and net employment connects the paper to two strands of private equity research. Davis et al. (2014) show that private equity buyouts can involve substantial job creation and destruction even when net employment effects are comparatively modest. Their outcomes are job flows across establishments; ours are worker entries and exits at the firm level, so the empirical objects are not interchangeable. More recent work shows that real-side responses also vary with transaction type, market conditions, and sponsor characteristics (Davis et al. 2025). We add a target-side dimension: recent hiring activity before investment distinguishes where subsequent worker-inflow responses are concentrated.

This target-side dimension is descriptive rather than structural. Low recent hiring can arise from different underlying conditions. Financing constraints are one possibility: private equity can provide capital or facilitate investment at firms whose prior expansion was constrained (Boucly et al. 2011). Governance changes, managerial intervention, recruiting capacity, or product-market opportunities provide others (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009; Gompers et al. 2016). Non-convex labour-adjustment costs also give a reason recent low activity could precede larger subsequent adjustment. The observed state does not distinguish these mechanisms, and we do not interpret it as a structural inaction regime or a persistent firm type.

Observable financial characteristics reinforce that limitation. In the audited subsample, pre-deal cash relative to assets, leverage, interest coverage, and profitability do not exhibit comparably clear associations with the event-level hiring response, whereas the hiring-state coefficient remains positive in the smaller sample. These financial variables are imperfect measures of financing constraints, however, and the audited subsample is more selected than the pension-based sample. Their weak associations do not eliminate financing as a channel.

The more consequential limitation is forward-looking sponsor selection. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring target because it anticipates future demand, a customer contract, financing, a management change, or another development that is not observed in the pension, transaction, or financial data. A matched untreated firm can share the same observed hiring history without sharing this information. Untreated pseudo-events address ordinary state-dependent rebound, and the pre-deal diagnostics do not reveal a positive divergence of comparable magnitude before investment. These exercises make simple mean reversion or continuation of an already visible trend less plausible as complete explanations. They cannot rule out information that remains latent until the investment occurs.

The external scope is similarly bounded. The matched population consists primarily of smaller Korean private firms and includes both minority growth investments and transactions involving control. It does not represent only leveraged buyouts, nor does it necessarily describe large public-to-private transactions or private equity investments in different labour-market and reporting environments. Our conclusions apply to the firms for which the administrative history and untreated support permit the matched design.

Within these limits, the paper makes three contributions. First, it documents a pronounced target-side dimension of post-private-equity labour adjustment: recent low-hiring targets exhibit much larger worker-inflow responses than recent high-hiring targets relative to state-balanced controls. Second, it separates that inflow heterogeneity from net employment growth; the targets with the largest inflow responses do not show correspondingly larger net-employment gains. Third, it shows why the counterfactual must be examined along the same pre-treatment dimension used to organise heterogeneity when that dimension predicts untreated outcome dynamics. The empirical contribution is therefore about where the post-investment response is concentrated and how that concentration should be benchmarked, not about identifying a unique organisational mechanism or an unconditional causal effect of private equity ownership.

# 2. Related literature and empirical motivation

## 2.1 Private equity, employment, and heterogeneous adjustment

Research on private equity shows that employment responses after investment are neither uniform across firms nor fully summarised by changes in the employment stock. Davis et al. (2014) document relatively modest net employment changes alongside much larger gross job creation and destruction around U.S. private equity buyouts. More recent evidence shows that real-side outcomes vary with transaction type, market conditions, and private equity sponsor characteristics (Davis et al. 2025). These findings shift the empirical question away from a single employment effect and toward the margins and sources of heterogeneity in post-investment adjustment.

Evidence from other institutional settings also points to variation in labour and operating outcomes after private equity transactions. Studies have documented post-buyout growth among French targets, changes in the composition of employment following Swedish buyouts, and worker-level consequences in German transactions (Boucly et al. 2011; Olsson and Tåg 2017; Antoni et al. 2019). Evidence from Asia includes employment outcomes following Japanese buyouts and operating outcomes around Korean restructuring transactions (Blind and Lottanti von Mandach 2021; Koo 2016). These studies differ in transaction coverage, institutional environment, data frequency, and the labour outcomes they can observe.

The Korean administrative setting provides a complementary measurement opportunity. Monthly pension records allow us to observe insured-worker entries, exits, and employment for predominantly private firms before and after investment. This frequency matters because recent hiring activity can be measured separately from the period used to construct the post-investment response. It also permits worker movement to be distinguished from changes in the employment stock.

That distinction should not be confused with the job-flow measures used in establishment-based research. Job creation and destruction refer to changes in employment positions across establishments or firms; worker flows record movements of individuals into and out of an employer. The two are related through employment accounting but need not coincide. A firm can replace departing workers or change workforce composition while maintaining a similar employment stock, and it can increase employment while simultaneously experiencing substantial worker turnover. We therefore use the evidence on gross job adjustment in prior private equity work as motivation for looking beyond net employment, not as a direct counterpart to the worker-entry and separation measures in our data.

A growing private equity literature also studies worker outcomes beyond headcount, including career paths, workplace safety, pay dispersion, labour-market power, and implicit employment relationships (Agrawal and Tambe 2016; Cohn et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2026; Herkenhoff et al. 2025). Our question is different. We ask why the realised labour response varies across targets and focus on a pre-treatment characteristic of the target rather than a post-treatment worker outcome.

Most evidence on heterogeneous private equity outcomes has naturally emphasised the deal and investor. Transaction structure captures variation in control and ownership, financing conditions shape the environment in which investment occurs, and sponsor characteristics can proxy for strategy, organisational capital, or experience. The target itself, however, can also enter the transaction from very different recent operating positions. Two targets receiving broadly similar forms of investment can differ sharply in whether they had been adding workers intensively or had undertaken little hiring in the preceding year.

Recent hiring condition is therefore a different organising dimension from transaction form or sponsor identity. It does not compete with these dimensions as a structural explanation. Rather, it asks whether observed target history contains information about where subsequent labour adjustment is concentrated. The paper’s empirical contribution is to document that target-side ordering and to examine the counterfactual problem created when the same ordering also appears among untreated firms.

## 2.2 Why pre-deal hiring may matter

Low pre-deal hiring can reflect several economically distinct conditions. A firm may have weak product demand, limited financing capacity, little recent need for workforce expansion, difficulty recruiting, or organisational constraints that delay adjustment. Private equity investment can coincide with changes in several of these margins. The same observed hiring history can therefore arise from different latent conditions, and the empirical state is not intended to identify one of them.

Financing capacity provides one possible interpretation. Boucly et al. (2011) report stronger growth following French leveraged buyouts among firms more likely to have faced financial constraints and interpret the evidence as consistent with buyouts relaxing those constraints. Private equity more generally can alter access to capital and firms’ ability to finance investment or expansion (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009). If low hiring partly reflects limited capacity to fund expansion, investment could be followed by a larger hiring response when that constraint changes.

The observable financial evidence in our setting does not isolate this channel. In the audited sample, a one-standard-deviation difference in pre-deal cash relative to assets is associated with a hiring-response coefficient of 0.0255 [-0.0650, 0.1152]; the corresponding leverage coefficient is -0.0045 [-0.1027, 0.0875], interest coverage 0.0489 [-0.0420, 0.0891], and profitability -0.0055 [-0.0911, 0.1018]. The pre-deal hiring-state coefficient remains positive within the audited sample, at 0.1087 [0.0229, 0.1964]. These comparisons show that the particular accounting measures do not provide a comparably clear ordering of the realised hiring response in the smaller audited sample. They are not direct measures of financing constraints and do not establish that financing is unimportant.

Governance and operational changes provide other possible interpretations. Private equity sponsors can alter managerial incentives, monitoring, operating practices, recruiting capacity, and growth strategy (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009; Gompers et al. 2016). A firm that has undertaken relatively little hiring before investment may have greater scope for subsequent recruiting or organisational adjustment. A firm already hiring intensively may have less scope for a further proportional increase over the same horizon. These possibilities motivate recent hiring history as an empirical state without implying that the observed worker-flow response identifies which intervention generated it.

Non-convex adjustment costs offer a related conceptual motivation. If labour adjustment involves fixed, organisational, or other nonlinear costs, firms need not change their workforces continuously as underlying conditions evolve. Periods of limited adjustment can be followed by larger changes when the benefits of adjustment rise sufficiently (Cooper et al. 2015). Persistent low hiring before the transaction can be viewed through this lens as a period of limited labour adjustment. We do not estimate such a structural model, and the empirical state should not be interpreted as an identified inaction band.

A simpler explanation requires no private equity mechanism at all. Low-hiring firms may simply revert toward their normal level of activity. A firm can pass through a temporary hiring pause or unusually weak period and then increase hiring later. Because the main outcome is measured relative to prior hiring activity, this rebound can generate a positive state gradient even among untreated firms. A positive relationship between low prior hiring and subsequent hiring is therefore insufficient evidence for a private-equity-related response.

This observation changes the relevant empirical benchmark. The null of interest is not a zero state gradient. It is the distribution of gradients produced by untreated firms that enter comparable observable states and are passed through the same empirical procedure. The untreated pseudo-event analysis is designed to estimate this background state dependence directly.

The same logic applies to matching. Suppose that low-hiring untreated firms rebound more strongly than high-hiring untreated firms. If matching ignores the hiring state, the controls assigned to different targets can themselves generate a slope with respect to the treated firm’s state. A heterogeneous treated-minus-control estimate then reflects two objects: how target outcomes vary with the state and how the matched counterfactual varies with it. The primary design therefore balances the state when constructing controls and separately reports the control-side slope as a diagnostic.

Recent hiring history can also be distinguished from a temporary pause at closing. If the primary relationship mainly reflects firms that happen to be between hiring episodes, the length of the no-entry spell already underway at the transaction should contain much of the same information. If the state instead summarises a broader recent condition, hiring activity over the preceding year should be more informative than the duration of the current pause. Conversely, a hiring measure taken two to three years before the transaction should be less informative if the relevant condition is relatively recent rather than a persistent firm type. Section 6 and Online Appendix B examine these distinctions.

Pre-deal employment growth provides another related comparison. A target may have low worker entries because employment is already contracting or because recent expansion has stalled. We therefore evaluate the hiring-state relationship conditional on pre-deal employment growth. We also residualise the state with respect to size, industry, growth, age, and seasonality. These analyses help locate what the state summarises, but they cannot make it independent of all observable or unobservable firm conditions.

The most difficult alternative is forward-looking selection by the sponsor. A sponsor may select a target after learning about future demand, an upcoming contract, financing, a management change, or another development not yet visible in observed worker flows. Such information need not produce a detectable pre-deal trend. Matching on the observed hiring state cannot eliminate this possibility, and an untreated pseudo-event distribution cannot reproduce information available only to the sponsor.

The empirical analysis therefore distinguishes three issues that should not be conflated. The first is ordinary rebound among low-hiring firms, which can be measured using untreated pseudo-events. The second is whether the state primarily reflects a short pause or a broader recent hiring condition, which can be examined using alternative pre-deal measures and windows. The third is forward-looking selection on unobserved information, which remains a limitation even if the first two explanations receive little support.

Our interpretation follows from these distinctions. We use the pre-deal hiring state as an organising variable for realised post-investment heterogeneity, not as a structural measure of financial constraints, organisational slack, or a permanent firm type. The empirical question is whether private equity targets entering from low-hiring states display worker-inflow responses that are unusually large relative to firms entering comparable observable states without a private equity investment, and whether those responses are accompanied by correspondingly larger changes in net employment. # 3. Institutional setting and data

## 3.1 National Pension records and worker-flow measurement

We use monthly administrative records from Korea’s National Pension Scheme (NPS) to measure insured employment and worker flows. For each reporting workplace, the records identify entries and exits of NPS-insured workers and the number of insured employees. The panel runs from November 2015 through May 2026. Its monthly frequency allows us to measure a target’s hiring condition well before a private equity investment and to distinguish subsequent worker inflows and exits from changes in the employment stock.

The primary state and outcome windows do not overlap. We measure the target’s pre-deal hiring state over months $-24$ through $-13$. The baseline component of the primary outcome uses months $-12$ through $-1$, and the post-investment component uses months $+1$ through $+12$. Worker entries used to define the state therefore do not enter the baseline hiring outcome mechanically. The transaction month is excluded from the pre/post comparison.

NPS reporting is administered at the workplace (*saeopjang*) level, whereas transactions are linked to firms through business registration numbers. A business can operate more than one registered workplace. We aggregate the pension records to the business-registration-number level and use that identifier as the empirical firm unit. We also track changes in the number of registered workplaces around the transaction because consolidation, re-registration, or another administrative reorganisation could otherwise appear as worker movement in the pension records. Online Appendix C examines this issue directly.

An NPS entry is interpreted throughout the paper as an administrative insured-worker inflow. After this qualification, we use “hire” and “hiring” as shorthand for the corresponding empirical measures. The administrative record does not independently identify a recruiting decision: an entry can occasionally reflect worker re-registration, payroll consolidation, or another change in pension reporting. This distinction is particularly relevant around changes in ownership. Online Appendix C therefore examines the accounting relation among worker entries, exits, and employment; excludes observations with large accounting discrepancies; removes firms with changes in registered-workplace counts; and reconstructs worker inflows from employment changes and recorded separations without using the reported entry field.

The records measure insured workers rather than all labour inputs. They do not observe workers outside NPS coverage, informal employment, hours worked, occupations, job vacancies, recruiting expenditures, or the managerial decision that generated an entry or exit. Our outcomes should consequently be interpreted as flows of NPS-insured workers and insured employment rather than complete measures of labour input or recruiting activity.

The NPS records also contain the contribution base used to calculate pension contributions. Because this base is subject to statutory bounds, it measures pensionable earnings rather than total compensation. We use this information only in supplementary analysis reported in Online Appendix F.

Monthly worker entries and exits are conceptually distinct from the gross job-flow measures used in establishment-based research. Worker flows record individuals entering and leaving an employer; job creation and destruction describe changes in employment positions. A firm can record substantial worker entries and exits while its employment stock changes little. This distinction is useful in our setting because the main results show considerably stronger heterogeneity in worker inflows than in net employment growth.

## 3.2 Private equity investments, sample construction, and supplementary data

We identify private equity investments in Korean firms from PitchBook and link the transactions to the NPS records using business registration numbers. Of the 379 matched events, 283 link directly by business registration number and 96 enter through the manually reviewed name-based recovery; candidate name matches judged ambiguous in that review were rejected and are documented in the linkage audit of the replication package. The treatment definition follows the private equity classification used to construct the transaction sample and is not restricted to leveraged buyouts or changes in control. The sample includes minority growth investments as well as transactions involving control. We therefore use *private equity investment* for the treated events generally and reserve *buyout* or *control transaction* for the relevant subsets.

We begin with 752 identified private equity investment events. Of these, 168 cannot be linked to the NPS records, 120 lack sufficient pre-investment history, 75 have fewer than five insured employees at the transaction, and 10 have no eligible firm in the baseline control cell. These restrictions leave 379 events in the baseline matched universe.

The average-effect and heterogeneity analyses form separate branches of this baseline universe. The average-effect analysis contains 367 events satisfying its outcome-availability rule. Conventional heterogeneity matching without balance on the hiring state yields 301 events. The primary state-balanced gradient design contains 286 events.

The restrictions from the 379-event baseline universe to the 286-event primary gradient sample can be accounted for directly. Fifty-three events do not have the complete months $-24$ through $-13$ state window, one falls below five insured employees within that state window, seventeen have no eligible state-balanced control cell, and twenty-two do not yield a usable primary log-hiring outcome under the complete-window and positive-entry requirements. These restrictions sum to the 93 events that do not enter the primary state-balanced analysis. The 301-event conventional heterogeneity sample should not be interpreted as an intermediate attrition stage between 379 and 286; it is generated by a different matching design.

The 379 baseline treated events correspond to distinct business registration numbers, so the target sample does not contain repeated transactions for the same empirical firm unit. Sponsor identities can recur across targets, an issue considered separately in Online Appendix E.

The primary state-gradient sample contains transactions from November 2017 through May 2025. Its timing is constrained by the requirement that both the earlier state window and the complete twelve-month post-investment window be observed. The baseline matched universe is less restrictive because construction of a match does not itself require a complete post-investment outcome window.

Sample selection also defines the population to which the estimates apply. Targets in the baseline matched design have median insured employment of 79.8 workers and mean employment of 218.2. Identified targets that do not enter the matched design have median employment of 135.6. The matched population therefore lies toward the smaller end of the observed target universe. The median transaction year is 2021, and the matched targets span 44 two-digit industries.

Table 1 reports sample construction and covariate balance for the primary analysis. In the 286-event state-balanced sample, treated firms and their controls are close in pre-deal size, employment growth, hiring state, prior hiring rate, and prior separation rate. The largest reported normalised difference is for firm age, at $-0.1676$. These balance statistics describe the observed covariates entering or closely related to the matching design; they do not establish balance on unobserved determinants of investment or subsequent hiring.

We supplement the NPS panel with audited annual financial statements and shareholder records. The financial statements provide cash, assets, liabilities, interest expense, revenue, and operating income. Statutory audit coverage produces a smaller and generally larger-firm subsample, so financial-statement availability is not required for inclusion in the main worker-flow analysis. The one-year audited analysis contains 147 events.

Annual shareholder records identify large shareholders and ownership stakes. We use them to measure acquired ownership, distinguish majority from minority investments where possible, and independently corroborate the year in which a private equity sponsor first appears as a shareholder. Their annual frequency does not provide a substitute for transaction-month dating in the monthly event study.

# 4. Research design

## 4.1 Matching and the pre-deal hiring state

For the baseline comparison, we match each private equity target to five firms that are never observed as private equity targets in the transaction data. Controls must be observed in the same event month and belong to the same exact cell defined by two-digit industry, firm-size bin, pre-deal employment-growth bin, and firm-age bin. All 752 identified private equity targets are excluded from the control pool, including targets that do not ultimately enter the matched analysis.

Firm size is measured as mean insured employment over months $-6$ through $-1$ and divided at 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 250 employees. Pre-deal employment growth compares employment over months $-6$ through $-1$ with employment over months $-18$ through $-13$ and is grouped using cutoffs at $-10$ and $+10$ percent. Firm age is measured from first observed NPS enrolment and grouped at five and fifteen years. Within an eligible exact cell, the five nearest controls are selected using log pre-deal employment and pre-deal employment growth. The neighbour distance is $((\Delta\log\text{employment})/0.9)^2+((\Delta\text{growth})/0.35)^2$, with growth clipped to $[-1,2]$ before differencing.

A target without an eligible untreated firm in its exact cell is excluded rather than matched outside the observed region of support. The resulting comparison restricts attention to targets for which observably similar untreated firms exist. Matching does not make private equity investment random, and the identifying assumptions therefore remain stronger than observed covariate balance alone.

The primary heterogeneity analysis adds the target’s recent hiring condition to this matching structure. Let $N_i^{state}$ denote worker entries over months $-24$ through $-13$, and let $E_i^{state}$ denote mean insured employment over the same twelve state-window months. We define

$$
S_i
=
-\log\left(
1+\frac{N_i^{state}}{E_i^{state}}
\right).
$$

Higher values of $S_i$ indicate lower pre-deal hiring intensity. The negative transformation is used so that a positive state gradient corresponds to a larger subsequent hiring response among targets that had been hiring less actively.

The state window is deliberately separated from the baseline outcome window. $S_i$ uses months $-24$ through $-13$, while the pre-investment hiring outcome uses months $-12$ through $-1$. This separation prevents the same worker entries from mechanically determining both the moderator and the pre-period component of the outcome.

For the primary heterogeneity design, we divide the state into terciles and add the tercile indicator to the exact matching cell. We then select five controls within that state-balanced cell using the same nearest-neighbour procedure. The continuous value of $S_i$, not the tercile indicator, is used to estimate the response gradient. The tercile restriction affects the construction of the comparison group rather than discretising the final heterogeneity estimand.

The reason for balancing the moderator follows from the estimand. Suppose untreated firms with low prior hiring experience stronger subsequent rebound than untreated firms with high prior hiring. Matching on industry, size, growth, and age can still assign controls whose subsequent outcomes vary systematically with the treated target’s state. A regression of the treated-minus-control response on $S_i$ can then combine two sources of variation: heterogeneity in the target’s outcome and heterogeneity in the counterfactual assigned to targets at different values of $S_i$.

This problem is distinct from ordinary imbalance in the mean of the moderator. The relevant question is whether the constructed untreated outcome path depends on the same pre-treatment variable used to organise treatment-response heterogeneity. Section 5.2 evaluates this directly by decomposing the gradient into treated- and control-side components under conventional and state-balanced matching.

State balancing addresses this observable moderator dependence. It does not imply that targets and controls share the same unobserved growth opportunities, financing conditions, managerial plans, or information sets. In particular, the design cannot eliminate selection on target-specific information available to the private equity sponsor but absent from our data.

## 4.2 Outcomes and the state-gradient estimand

For a firm $i$ and twelve-month window $w$, define the hiring rate as

$$
HR_{iw}
=
\frac{N_{iw}}{\bar E_{iw}},
$$

where $N_{iw}$ is the total number of NPS worker entries during the window and $\bar E_{iw}$ is mean insured employment over the same months. The measure is not annualised.

The primary pre-investment window covers months $-12$ through $-1$, and the post-investment window covers months $+1$ through $+12$. For target $i$, let $C(i)$ denote its matched control firms. The event-level matched response is

$$
D_i
=
\left[
\log HR_{i,post}
-
\log HR_{i,pre}
\right]
-
\frac{1}{|C(i)|}
\sum_{j\in C(i)}
\left[
\log HR_{j,post}
-
\log HR_{j,pre}
\right].
$$

A positive $D_i$ means that the target’s hiring rate increased more, or declined less, than the mean change among its matched controls.

The primary heterogeneity estimand is the gradient of $D_i$ with respect to the target’s continuous pre-deal state. We partial out log pre-deal size, pre-deal employment growth, firm age, and one-digit industry from both $D_i$ and $S_i$. Let $\widetilde D_i$ and $\widetilde S_i$ denote the corresponding residuals. We estimate

$$
\widetilde D_i
=
\beta \widetilde S_i
+
\varepsilon_i.
$$

Because higher $S_i$ indicates lower pre-deal hiring intensity, a positive $\beta$ means that targets entering from lower-hiring states exhibit larger post-investment changes in hiring relative to their state-balanced controls.

The coefficient is a cross-target state gradient in the matched response. It is not the average post-investment effect reported in Section 5.1. Nor is it a structural parameter describing a labour-adjustment friction or a forecasting coefficient for future transactions.

The primary gradient requires complete twelve-month pre- and post-investment outcome windows and positive hiring rates so that the logarithm is defined. The log-hiring restriction removes three events that record zero worker entries in one of the outcome windows. Online Appendix B reports inverse-hyperbolic-sine and $\log(1+HR)$ transformations that retain zero-entry windows.

The average-effect analysis follows a different availability rule. It requires at least six observed months in each relevant pre- and post-investment window for the target and an eligible control, yielding 367 events. The primary heterogeneity design imposes the complete state and outcome requirements described above and yields 286 events. Results from the two samples therefore answer different empirical questions and are not presented as estimates on a common population.

The primary state-gradient specification winsorises the event-level response at the 5th and 95th percentiles of the treated sample. The same absolute cutoffs are then applied to pseudo-event outcomes when constructing the untreated reference distribution. Online Appendix B reports unwinsorised estimates, alternative percentile cutoffs, alternative transformations, and alternative matching and weighting procedures.

Because employment appears in the denominator of $HR$, we separately estimate the state gradient in the raw log number of worker entries. We also report a diagnostic specification that includes the post-investment change in log employment. The latter is not interpreted as a causal control because employment is itself a post-investment outcome. Its purpose is limited to assessing whether the hiring-rate gradient is generated primarily by movements in the employment denominator.

Net employment is measured directly using the employment stock rather than inferred from worker-entry rates. This distinction is important because larger worker inflows need not imply larger employment growth if worker exits or changes in the scale of the firm differ at the same time.

## 4.3 Untreated reference distribution and statistical inference

A zero-centred null is not an appropriate benchmark for the state gradient. Firms with unusually low hiring can increase hiring subsequently even without private equity investment. Mean reversion, temporary hiring pauses, and other state-dependent untreated dynamics can therefore generate a positive relation between $S_i$ and subsequent hiring in the absence of a private equity event.

We estimate this background relationship directly from untreated firms. Each eligible untreated firm is assigned a pseudo-event date and processed through the same state construction, matching, outcome transformation, covariate adjustment, and winsorisation procedure used for private equity targets. This procedure yields a pool of 1,205 untreated pseudo-events for the primary state-gradient specification. For each of 2,000 reference draws, we randomly order the untreated pseudo-event cells, accumulate pseudo-events by cell until the treated-sample size is reached, retain 286 observations, and re-estimate the state gradient using the primary transformation and covariate adjustment, producing an empirical untreated reference distribution.

The reference distribution is not forced to have mean zero. It captures the state-dependent rebound and other features of the empirical procedure that arise even when the focal private equity event is absent. The relevant comparison is therefore whether the target gradient is unusual relative to gradients generated by untreated firms processed through the same design, not whether the target coefficient differs from zero.

We do not interpret this distribution as a randomisation distribution. Private equity treatment is not randomly assigned, and the pseudo-events do not reproduce an experimental assignment mechanism. The exercise calibrates the observed target gradient against untreated dynamics; it cannot reproduce unobserved target-specific information that may influence private equity selection.

For the primary specification, the 2,000-draw untreated reference distribution has mean 0.1010 and standard deviation 0.1538. The observed target gradient of 0.7101 lies 3.96 reference-distribution standard deviations above that mean. The empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.0005. The reported probability equals $(1+\#\{b:|g_b-\bar g|\ge|g^{obs}-\bar g|\})/(B+1)$ over the $B=2{,}000$ specification-specific draws, where $\bar g$ is the draw mean; ties count toward the numerator, and the add-one correction bounds the probability below by $1/(B+1)=0.0005$. We report the empirical probability separately from the bootstrap confidence interval because the two quantities answer different questions.

Uncertainty in the target gradient is estimated by resampling treated events together with their matched comparison sets. The primary analysis uses 2,000 bootstrap draws and yields a 95 percent interval of [0.3187, 1.1254]. The treated observations are distinct firms.

Control reuse is limited. The 286 primary events use 1,144 distinct control firms; 95.02 percent of these controls appear in only one matched event, and no control appears in more than three. Online Appendix D reports an alternative control-firm bootstrap and a specification assigning each control to at most one event. We retain the matched-event bootstrap because the treated event and its comparison set constitute the unit from which the event-level response is constructed.

The primary state-gradient analysis and the untreated reference comparison address an observable form of state-dependent counterfactual movement. A causal interpretation requires the stronger condition that, after matching on the observed characteristics and hiring state, the matched controls approximate the state-specific path the target would have followed without private equity investment. That condition cannot be tested directly. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring target because it anticipates demand, a contract, financing, management changes, or another future development not yet visible in the administrative data. # 5. Main results

## 5.1 Average post-investment changes

We begin with average changes relative to matched controls to establish the scale of post-investment labour adjustment before turning to heterogeneity across pre-deal hiring states.

Employment rises relative to matched controls. Twelve months after the transaction, the treated-minus-control change in log employment is 0.0878 [0.0462, 0.1300], corresponding to roughly 9 percent. The matched change in the hiring rate between the twelve months before and after investment is 0.0485 [0.0163, 0.0814], or 4.85 percentage points. Both estimates use the 367-event average-effect sample.

The separation rate changes much less on average. Its matched pre-to-post change is 0.0007 [-0.0353, 0.0398]. The interval accommodates changes in either direction and should not be interpreted as evidence that separations are unchanged.

These estimates provide a benchmark for the state-gradient analysis. Private equity targets in the matched population increase employment and worker inflows on average, but the average response does not indicate whether the same targets account for the largest changes in both outcomes.

## 5.2 Hiring responses across pre-deal hiring states

The average increase in hiring masks substantial cross-target variation. In the primary state-balanced sample, targets entering the transaction with lower pre-deal hiring intensity exhibit larger subsequent increases in hiring relative to their matched controls.

The continuous state gradient is 0.7101, with a 95 percent treated-event bootstrap interval of [0.3187, 1.1254] (Table 3, Panel A). An interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.2005-log-point larger matched response [0.0900, 0.3177]. On the corresponding multiplicative scale, this is approximately a 22 percent larger relative hiring-rate response. The magnitude describes variation across target states; it is not the average effect of private equity investment.

Low-hiring untreated firms also rebound. The untreated reference distribution has a mean gradient of 0.1010 and a standard deviation of 0.1538. The observed target gradient exceeds the untreated mean by 0.6091 log points and lies 3.96 reference-distribution standard deviations above it. The empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.0005. The relevant result is therefore not simply that firms with low prior hiring subsequently increase hiring. Rather, the gradient among private equity targets is unusually large relative to the state-dependent hiring movements generated by untreated firms processed through the same empirical procedure.

Figure 1(a) displays the event-level relationship between the matched hiring response and the target’s pre-deal state. Figure 1(b) plots the empirical distribution of the 2,000 untreated-reference gradients rather than a fitted parametric approximation. The target estimate lies in the far upper tail of that empirical distribution.

The way controls are constructed is consequential. On the 286 target events common to both procedures, conventional matching without state balance produces a gradient of 0.4698. Adding the hiring-state tercile to the exact matching cell produces the primary gradient of 0.7101 (Table 3, Panel B).

Panel C examines why the two designs differ. The decomposition uses the same treated events and an unwinsorised outcome. The treated-side slope is therefore mechanically identical across the two matching procedures, at 0.8425. The informative difference appears in the assigned controls. Under conventional matching, the control-side outcome has a slope of 0.2950 with respect to the treated target’s state. Under state-balanced matching, this slope falls to 0.0056.

The conventional comparison therefore assigns systematically different subsequent control paths to targets in different hiring states. Balancing the moderator removes most of that observable dependence. The result does not imply that state-balanced controls reproduce the untreated counterfactual under all forms of selection; it shows that one identifiable source of moderator-dependent counterfactual movement is materially reduced.

Panel C is unwinsorised, whereas Panel B reports the preferred 5/95-winsorised gradients. The component slopes in Panel C therefore do not subtract numerically to the coefficients in Panel B. Their role is diagnostic: they locate the source of the difference between the two matching procedures.

The positive state relationship also survives a range of alternative specifications, although its magnitude is not invariant. Increasing the number of controls to twenty gives a gradient of 0.678, adding the continuous state to the neighbour-distance calculation gives 0.640, and adding deal-year fixed effects gives 0.678. Inverse-variance weighting yields a smaller estimate of 0.346. Online Appendix B reports these and other specification sensitivities. The collection of estimates supports a positive state-dependent pattern while showing that the magnitude depends on how events are weighted and how the pre-deal state is measured.

## 5.3 Worker inflows and net employment

The state gradient remains when worker entries are measured without employment in the denominator. This matters because a ratio outcome can otherwise change through either its numerator or its denominator.

Panel A of Table 4 holds the state-balanced design and event sample fixed while varying the worker-inflow outcome. The primary log hiring-rate gradient is 0.7101, with an outcome-specific untreated reference mean of 0.1006 and an empirical two-sided $p$-value of 0.0005. Replacing the rate with the raw log worker-entry count yields a gradient of 0.6315. Its untreated reference mean is 0.1858 and its empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.0110.

A further diagnostic includes the change in log employment while using the log worker-entry count as the outcome. The resulting gradient is 0.6957, against an untreated reference mean of 0.0377, with an empirical two-sided $p$-value of 0.0005. Because employment is itself a post-investment outcome, this specification is not a causal adjustment. Its purpose is narrower: the positive state relationship is present in worker-entry counts even when the hiring-rate denominator is removed.

Net employment follows a different pattern. The state gradient in log employment is $-0.0580$, with a standardized distance of $-2.60$ relative to its outcome-specific untreated reference distribution and a two-sided empirical $p$-value of 0.005. Thus, targets with lower pre-deal hiring intensity exhibit larger worker-inflow responses but smaller relative net-employment responses along the same state dimension.

This negative employment gradient does not contradict the positive average employment change in Table 2. The two coefficients answer different questions. The average result shows that target employment rises relative to matched controls across the 367-event average-effect sample. The gradient shows how the relative employment response varies across pre-deal hiring states. Targets can therefore increase employment on average while lower-hiring targets experience smaller employment gains than higher-hiring targets.

Worker churn also has a positive state gradient. The estimate is 0.3816, 3.08 reference-distribution standard deviations above its untreated mean, with a two-sided empirical $p$-value of 0.0020. Churn includes worker entries as well as separations, however, so this result is not independent evidence of a stronger exit response.

The separation gradient is 0.1788, with a standardized distance of 1.17 and an empirical two-sided $p$-value of 0.2384. The point estimate is positive, but the available precision does not establish a distinct state-dependent separation margin. We therefore do not interpret the results as identification of worker replacement or two-sided reallocation.

Within-event contrasts sharpen the distinction between worker inflows and net employment. On the common 286 events, the hiring-minus-employment gradient is 0.7536, compared with an untreated reference mean of 0.0232. It lies 4.56 reference-distribution standard deviations above that mean, with an empirical two-sided $p$-value of 0.001. The churn-minus-employment contrast is 0.4539 across 288 events, with an untreated reference mean of $-0.0750$, a standardized distance of 4.19, and an empirical two-sided $p$-value of 0.001.

The strongest conclusion from these outcomes is therefore about worker inflows relative to net employment. Low-hiring targets display unusually large increases in insured-worker inflows, while their relative net-employment gains are smaller. Churn moves in the same broad direction, but the separation evidence does not support a stronger claim that private equity induces a distinct state-dependent exit or replacement mechanism.

# 6. Validity, interpretation, and remaining selection

## 6.1 Untreated rebound and pre-deal hiring dynamics

The primary comparison addresses observable differences in the matched control group, but it does not by itself establish that private equity investment causes the larger hiring response among low-hiring targets. Two alternative explanations can be examined using pre-treatment and untreated outcomes. Low-hiring firms may rebound even without private equity investment, and eventual private equity targets may already be moving onto different hiring paths before the transaction.

The untreated reference distribution addresses the first concern. Its mean state gradient is about 0.101, compared with 0.7101 among private equity targets. Low-hiring untreated firms therefore do exhibit subsequent rebound, but the magnitude is much smaller than the target gradient. Ordinary state-dependent recovery is unlikely to account for the observed target gradient on its own. The exercise does not address forces specific to firms subsequently selected for private equity investment.

We next ask whether a similar positive divergence is visible before investment. The primary state is measured over months $-24$ through $-13$, which creates a trade-off in constructing a pre-deal diagnostic. A mechanically non-overlapping comparison requires moving the state window further into the past; retaining the primary state preserves moderator comparability but causes the state and earlier outcome to share observations.

Our first diagnostic adopts the non-overlapping approach. We move the state window to months $-36$ through $-25$ and measure the earlier outcome separately. Relative to the corresponding untreated reference, the pre-deal excess gradient is $-0.1770$ [-0.4394, 0.0854] across 250 events (Table 5). The interval does not contain a positive divergence approaching the post-investment primary gradient of 0.7101. This is the mechanically cleaner comparison, but it does not use the same moderator as the primary specification.

The second diagnostic retains the primary state definition. Among the 283 events for which the comparison is available, the pre-deal gradient is 0.1068 [-0.1955, 0.4351]. On the same 283 events, the post-deal gradient is 0.7250 [0.3259, 1.1081]. The pre-deal point estimate is much smaller, but its interval remains compatible with a meaningful positive pre-existing relationship. This specification therefore retains moderator comparability at the cost of mechanical overlap.

The two results are complementary rather than interchangeable. The earlier-state specification provides a cleaner separation of state and outcome windows but changes the state being studied. The primary-state specification preserves the focal moderator but provides a less clean pre-deal comparison. Neither should be treated as a formal test that the identifying assumption is satisfied.

Quarterly estimates reported in Online Appendix D provide a higher-frequency view. The four treated pre-deal gradients are $+0.087$, $-0.042$, $-0.038$, and $-0.056$, with a mean of $-0.012$ [-0.163, 0.131]. A fitted linear pre-deal drift is $-0.038$ [-0.600, 0.473]. These estimates do not display a persistent positive ordering before investment, but they are too imprecise to exclude moderate differential dynamics.

Online Appendix D also reports relative-magnitude sensitivity calculations following Rambachan and Roth (2023). At the twelve-month resolution aligned with the primary state-gradient estimand, the breakdown value is 3.126. At quarterly frequency, the corresponding calculation is uninformative because the pre-period gradients are too noisy to provide a stable sensitivity bound. We treat both calculations as sensitivity calibrations rather than validation of the identifying assumption.

## 6.2 What does the pre-deal hiring state capture?

The primary state can reflect different features of a firm’s recent history. Online Appendix B distinguishes a broader period of low hiring from a short hiring pause already in progress when the private equity investment occurs.

Controlling for pre-deal employment growth leaves a positive state coefficient of 0.410 [0.078, 0.753]. The historical share of months with no worker entry is positively related to the post-investment response, with an estimate of 0.559 [0.241, 0.885]. By contrast, the duration of the no-entry spell already in progress at closing has an estimate of 0.040 [-0.016, 0.107]. When historical no-entry frequency and pause duration enter jointly, their corresponding coefficients are 0.536 and 0.032. The pattern is more consistent with the state summarising a broader recent hiring condition than merely the length of a temporary pause at the transaction date.

The state is not, however, a distinct structural firm characteristic. Size, industry, pre-deal employment growth, age, and seasonality jointly account for 63.9 percent of its variation. After removing that observable component, the residualised-state coefficient is 0.243 [-0.221, 0.723]. The point estimate remains positive, but the interval is wide. This specification therefore does not establish that the state contains a precise relationship with the subsequent response orthogonal to conventional observable characteristics.

A state measured further from the transaction is also less informative. Defining the state over months $-36$ through $-25$ gives a post-deal gradient of 0.268, compared with a specification-specific untreated mean of 0.141 and a standardized distance of 0.92. Together with the pause comparison, this attenuation is more consistent with the state measuring a relatively recent hiring condition than a time-invariant firm type.

Observable financial condition provides another comparison. In the audited subsample, financial variables are taken from the fiscal year preceding the deal year, clipped at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and standardised. A one-standard-deviation difference in cash relative to assets has a response coefficient of 0.0255 [-0.0650, 0.1152]. The corresponding coefficients are $-0.0045$ [-0.1027, 0.0875] for leverage, 0.0489 [-0.0420, 0.0891] for interest coverage, and $-0.0055$ [-0.0911, 0.1018] for profitability.

The hiring state remains positively associated with the outcome in the audited subsample: its coefficient is 0.1087 [0.0229, 0.1964] across 169 events. These coefficients are reported per standard deviation and use an unwinsorised event-level hiring-response outcome. The accounting measures do not provide a comparably clear ordering in these smaller audited samples.

This comparison does not reject a financing interpretation. Cash, leverage, interest coverage, and profitability are imperfect proxies for financing constraints, and audited firms form a more selected and generally larger subset of the matched population. The result establishes only that these particular observed financial measures do not reproduce the same clear cross-sectional ordering. Financing capacity, governance, product demand, managerial capacity, recruiting access, and other firm conditions remain possible explanations for why recent low-hiring targets respond differently.

## 6.3 Forward-looking sponsor selection and the causal boundary

The preceding diagnostics provide less leverage against a different form of selection: information held by the sponsor about the target’s future prospects. A private equity sponsor may invest in a low-hiring firm because it anticipates future demand, a large customer contract, access to financing, a management change, or another event that will later be accompanied by hiring. Observably similar untreated firms need not share that information.

Such selection need not generate an observable pre-deal trend. If the sponsor’s information has already affected employment or worker inflows before the transaction, the diagnostics in Section 6.1 can constrain the resulting divergence. If the information remains latent in observed outcomes until investment occurs, however, the absence of a large pre-deal gradient does not eliminate the selection channel.

Online Appendix D considers a narrower timing story using fund age as a proxy for deployment pressure. The point estimate runs opposite to the prediction that sponsors with greater timing discretion should display a larger response, but fund age is not randomly assigned and is not a direct measure of the sponsor’s target-specific information. The exercise therefore does not remove forward-looking selection.

Other auxiliary comparisons have similar limits. Shareholder records provide an independent check on the transaction year for a subset of events, but not exact transaction-month timing. Not-yet-treated firms provide an alternative comparison group but reduce support and can themselves be affected by anticipation of a future private equity transaction. Sponsor exits and non-private-equity ownership changes are sparse or economically different from the primary treatment. We use these analyses to describe the range of observable alternatives rather than as substitute identification strategies.

The evidence consequently supports a bounded interpretation of the primary result. Ordinary state-dependent rebound among untreated firms is much smaller than the target gradient, and the available pre-deal diagnostics do not reveal a positive divergence of comparable magnitude before investment. These findings make ordinary mean reversion and continuation of an already visible hiring trend less plausible as complete explanations.

They do not eliminate selection on information unavailable in the pension, transaction, shareholder, and financial data. We therefore interpret the primary estimate as an unusually large post-investment hiring response among low-hiring private equity targets relative to observably comparable untreated firms. Stronger causal attribution remains conditional on the absence of sufficiently large forward-looking sponsor selection.

# 7. Additional evidence and scope checks

## 7.1 Post-investment hiring dynamics

The average matched hiring difference appears soon after the transaction. In the quarterly specification, the first post-investment estimate is positive, with a 95 percent interval of [0.0032, 0.0250], and the subsequent point estimates remain positive through the tenth quarter. The first four post-investment quarterly estimates sum to approximately 0.048, close to the twelve-month hiring-rate change of 0.0485 reported in Table 2.

Figure 2 displays these quarterly differences. We use the estimates to describe the timing of the matched average response rather than as a quarterly version of the primary state-gradient analysis. Sample composition also changes at longer horizons as later transaction cohorts cease to have complete follow-up.

The monthly records additionally show that worker entries occur in a broader set of months after investment. That pattern can arise mechanically when total worker-entry volume increases. Holding the number of observed months fixed, a firm with more realised entries will generally have fewer months with no entries even if the allocation of entries across months otherwise follows the same process.

Online Appendix A therefore compares the observed monthly pattern with a volume-preserving allocation benchmark. The primary benchmark holds each firm-window’s realised worker-entry volume and monthly employment exposure fixed and reallocates entries across months in proportion to employment exposure.

The observed share of months with no worker entry falls by 0.0466. Under the employment-weighted volume benchmark, the predicted decline is 0.0508, leaving an excess change of 0.0042 [-0.0088, 0.0180]. The residual interval is small relative to the observed decline. An equal-month benchmark gives a similar conclusion: its predicted decline is 0.0518, leaving an excess of 0.0052 [-0.0082, 0.0189].

The broader monthly incidence of positive worker entries is therefore largely accounted for by increased realised entry volume under these benchmarks. We do not interpret the result as showing that private equity leaves recruiting timing unchanged. The benchmark does not model firm-specific seasonality, month-specific demand shocks, or other determinants of the within-window allocation of worker entries. It shows only that the observed decline in no-entry months does not require a comparably large additional change in monthly allocation once realised volume is taken into account.

The treatment sample contains both minority growth investments and transactions involving control. We therefore examine whether the event-level hiring response differs across these broad transaction forms.

# 8. Conclusion

This paper examines whether post-private-equity labour adjustment varies with the target’s recent hiring condition before investment. Using monthly administrative worker-flow records from Korea and a state-balanced matched design, we find a pronounced cross-target gradient. An interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity is associated with a 0.2005-log-point larger treated-minus-control change in the hiring rate.

Low-hiring untreated firms also rebound. That fact is central to the design rather than a nuisance to be removed by assuming a zero benchmark. When untreated firms are processed through the same state construction, matching, transformation, and estimation procedure, their mean gradient is much smaller than the 0.7101 gradient observed among private equity targets. The target estimate lies far in the upper range of the resulting empirical reference distribution.

The construction of the counterfactual matters for the estimated heterogeneity. Under conventional matching, the outcome path of the assigned controls varies systematically with the treated target’s state. Balancing the hiring state largely removes this control-side relationship while leaving the treated events unchanged. The setting therefore illustrates a design issue that extends beyond private equity: when a pre-treatment variable is used to organise heterogeneous responses and also predicts untreated outcome dynamics, the comparison group should be examined along that same dimension.

The economic margin is also specific. The state relationship remains in worker-entry counts that do not scale entries by employment. By contrast, the gradient in net employment is negative. Low-hiring targets therefore exhibit particularly large post-investment worker-inflow responses without correspondingly larger net-employment gains. Churn also rises more along the low-hiring dimension, but the separation gradient is imprecise. The data consequently locate the heterogeneity more clearly in worker inflows than in net employment, without identifying a distinct worker-exit or replacement mechanism.

The pre-deal hiring state should not be read as a structural latent state. It is related to conventional target characteristics, and residualising it on those characteristics substantially reduces precision. A state measured further in the past is less informative, while the duration of a temporary hiring pause at closing provides a weaker ordering than the broader recent history. The evidence is therefore more consistent with a relatively recent hiring condition than with a time-invariant firm type, but it does not reveal the underlying organisational or financial mechanism.

The design has a corresponding causal boundary. Untreated rebound and the available pre-deal dynamics make ordinary mean reversion and continuation of a comparably large visible trend less plausible as complete explanations. They cannot rule out forward-looking selection on information available to the private equity sponsor but absent from the data. A sponsor may select a low-hiring target precisely because it anticipates demand, financing, contracts, or managerial changes that will later generate worker inflows. The primary result is therefore an unusually large matched post-investment response among low-hiring targets, not an unconditional causal estimate of the effect of private equity ownership.

The population is also bounded by the empirical support of the design. The matched targets are concentrated among smaller Korean private firms and include both growth investments and transactions involving control. The results need not extend to large public-to-private buyouts or to institutional environments with different labour-market, pension-reporting, or private equity structures.

Within those limits, the evidence changes how post-private-equity employment heterogeneity is organised. Recent target hiring history identifies where the largest worker-inflow responses occur, while net employment alone would obscure that ordering. At the same time, the moderator’s relation with untreated paths shows that heterogeneity cannot be separated cleanly from the construction of the counterfactual. These two observations-the target-side concentration of the worker-inflow response and the need to benchmark it against state-dependent untreated dynamics-constitute the paper’s main empirical contribution.

# Appendix A. Monthly hiring allocation and the volume benchmark

The monthly data show that private equity targets record worker entries in a broader set of months after investment. Part of this pattern arises mechanically when total worker-entry volume increases. A firm that records more entries over a fixed horizon will generally have fewer months with no entries even if the within-window allocation of entries otherwise remains unchanged.

This appendix compares the observed monthly pattern with benchmarks that preserve realised worker-entry volume. The main employment-weighted benchmark also preserves monthly employment exposure. We then distinguish changes in monthly participation and concentration that are implied by increased volume from residual changes in the allocation of worker entries across months.

## A.1 Construction of the volume benchmark

For each firm-window, let $N$ denote the realised number of worker entries and let $E_j$ denote insured employment in month $j$. Define

$$
w_j
=
\frac{E_j}{\sum_k E_k},
$$

so that $w_j$ is month $j$'s share of total insured-employment exposure over the window.

The employment-weighted benchmark holds $N$, the set of observed months, and monthly employment exposure fixed and reallocates the $N$ entries across months with probabilities $w_j$. Under this allocation, the expected number of months with no worker entry is

$$
\sum_j (1-w_j)^N.
$$

We also report an equal-month benchmark. In a complete twelve-month window, its expected number of no-entry months is

$$
12\left(\frac{11}{12}\right)^N.
$$

The two rules are reported separately because employment is not constant across months. The employment-weighted calculation is the primary benchmark in the revised analysis; the equal-month calculation provides a simpler sensitivity comparison.

For measures without a closed-form expectation-the longest spell without a worker entry, the Herfindahl index of entries across months, and the share of entries occurring in the two busiest months-we simulate the corresponding multinomial allocation 100 times for each firm-window under a fixed seed (42), so the benchmark values are exactly reproducible. For the remaining simulated measures, the allocation probabilities follow the employment-exposure weights $w_j$.

For any monthly-allocation measure $M$, define the excess change as

$$
\Delta M^{excess}
=
\Delta M^{observed}
-
\Delta M^{benchmark}.
$$

The excess is therefore the component of the treated-minus-control change that is not implied by the corresponding volume-preserving allocation. It is not a regression coefficient obtained by conditioning on realised volume.

Before the transaction, treated firms record an average of 91.9 worker entries over a twelve-month window. They average 3.23 months with no worker entry, compared with 2.58 under the volume benchmark. Their longest no-entry spell averages 1.97 months, compared with 1.53 under the benchmark. The Herfindahl index of monthly worker entries is 0.203 in the data and 0.163 under the benchmark; the corresponding shares of entries occurring in the two busiest months are 0.468 and 0.378. Thus, worker entries are more concentrated across months than the volume-preserving allocation would predict even before investment.

## A.3 Decomposition of the average hiring increase

The primary twelve-month hiring rate is total worker entries divided by mean insured employment over the same window:

$$
HR
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12}N_j}{\bar E}
=
\frac{12\sum_{j=1}^{12}N_j}
{\sum_{j=1}^{12}E_j}.
$$

A decomposition based on the simple share of active months and the unweighted mean of monthly hiring intensity is not an exact identity when employment varies across months. We therefore define the active component using employment exposure.

Let

$$
A=\{j:N_j>0\}
$$

denote the set of months with positive worker entries, and define

$$
q
=
\frac{\sum_{j\in A}E_j}
{\sum_{j=1}^{12}E_j},
$$

the share of total employment exposure occurring in months with positive worker entries. Define active-month worker-entry intensity as

$$
i_E
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12}N_j}
{\sum_{j\in A}E_j}.
$$

These quantities satisfy the exact identity

$$
HR=12q i_E.
$$

Hence,

$$
\log HR
=
\log 12+\log q+\log i_E,
$$

and

$$
\Delta\log HR
=
\Delta\log q+\Delta\log i_E.
$$

The decomposition therefore separates the change in exposure accounted for by months with positive worker entries from the change in worker-entry intensity within those active months, while remaining algebraically consistent with the primary hiring-rate definition.

Relaxing the requirement that all twelve state months be observed increases the sample to 310 events and produces a gradient of 0.655. We retain complete state windows in the primary specification because allowing incomplete windows changes the measurement horizon of the moderator across events.

Using a longer state window from months $-36$ through $-13$ reduces the sample to 244 events and the gradient to 0.388. The result is consistent with the separate earlier-state analysis in Section B.2: hiring history measured further from the transaction provides a weaker ordering of subsequent responses.

The estimate is more sensitive to event weighting. Inverse-variance weighting reduces the gradient to 0.346, with a standardized distance of 2.28 from its specification-specific untreated reference mean. Adding deal-year fixed effects produces a gradient of 0.678 and a standardized distance of 3.50.

The alternatives therefore differ meaningfully in magnitude and precision. They support a positive relationship between lower recent pre-deal hiring intensity and the subsequent matched hiring response across a range of design choices, but not an estimator-invariant coefficient. The smaller inverse-variance and longer-state estimates form part of the substantive sensitivity of the result rather than exceptions to be discarded.

# Appendix C. Validation of administrative worker inflows and flow outcomes

The main analysis interprets monthly National Pension Scheme entries as administrative worker inflows. Two measurement issues warrant separate examination. First, an NPS entry need not correspond one-for-one to external recruitment. Worker re-registration, payroll consolidation, or other administrative changes can generate recorded entries around a transaction. Second, the hiring and separation rates scale worker flows by employment, which can itself change after investment. This appendix examines the accounting relation among entries, exits, and insured employment, evaluates restrictions designed to remove observations with greater measurement concerns, reconstructs worker inflows without using the reported entry field, and compares flow-based measures with the employment stock.

## C.1 Accounting consistency of worker entries and exits

Let $E_{it}$ denote insured employment for firm $i$ in month $t$, $Entry_{it}$ reported worker entries, and $Exit_{it}$ reported worker exits. We define the accounting discrepancy as

$$
d_{it}
=
(E_{it}-E_{i,t-1})
-
(Entry_{it}-Exit_{it}).
$$

If employment stocks and reported flows reconciled exactly under a common timing convention, $d_{it}=0$. In practice, discrepancies can arise because the stock is the month-end count of insured workers while entries and exits are the month's reported acquisition and loss records, so late or corrected filings can move the two apart.

We define the absolute relative discrepancy as

$$
r_{it}
=
\frac{|d_{it}|}
{E_{i,t-1}}.
$$

Across 227,339 firm-month observations, the median absolute discrepancy is one worker. Twenty-four point three percent of firm-months reconcile exactly. The median absolute discrepancy relative to employment is 1.4 percent, and the 90th percentile is 7.1 percent.

|---|---:|

The increase at the transaction month is modest but makes it inappropriate to assume perfect reconciliation precisely when ownership changes. The following analyses therefore examine whether the state gradient is sensitive to observations with comparatively large discrepancies and to other indicators of administrative restructuring.

Changes in the number of registered NPS workplaces provide a separate check. Only three of the 379 treated firms in the baseline matched universe change their registered-workplace count around the transaction. Workplace restructuring is therefore uncommon in the treated sample, although its rarity does not establish that every recorded entry represents an external recruit.

The accounting evidence supports using NPS entries as administrative worker-inflow measures while retaining the distinction made in Section 3.1 between an insured-worker entry and an independently observed recruiting decision.

## C.2 Measurement filters and reconstructed worker inflows

We next re-estimate the hiring-state gradient under increasingly restrictive measurement rules. The first two specifications remove firm-months with relatively large discrepancies between reported worker flows and employment changes. A third removes months with exceptionally high total turnover. A fourth excludes treated firms that change their number of registered workplaces around investment.

The final specification reconstructs worker inflows without using the reported entry variable. From the employment-flow identity,

$$
E_{it}-E_{i,t-1}
=
Entry_{it}-Exit_{it}+d_{it},
$$

the implied inflow absent the discrepancy term is

$$
Entry^{*}_{it}
=
(E_{it}-E_{i,t-1})+Exit_{it}.
$$

The implemented reconstructed measure follows

$$
Entry^{*}_{it}
=
\max\{0,\,(E_{it}-E_{i,t-1})+Exit_{it}\},
$$

with negative implied flows set to zero, missing or non-consecutive months invalidating the window, and the transaction month excluded because the windows are months $-12$ through $-1$ and $+1$ through $+12$. Pre- and post-investment reconstructed flows are summed within each window and divided by window-mean insured employment, entering the same gradient pipeline as the primary measure.

## D.2 Relative-magnitude sensitivity of the state gradient

We use the relative-magnitude framework of Rambachan and Roth (2023) to calibrate sensitivity to differential trends that may remain after matching.

Let $\delta_t$ denote the differential trend component at event time $t$. Under the relative-magnitude restriction,

$$
|\delta_{t+1}-\delta_t|
\leq
\bar M
\max_{s<0}
|\delta_{s+1}-\delta_s|.
$$

The parameter $\bar M$ therefore limits the largest post-treatment change in the differential trend relative to the largest corresponding change observed before treatment. Larger values permit larger departures from the pre-period pattern.

For the twelve-month implementation used in the primary analysis, we map the pre- and post-investment state gradients estimated on the same 283 events into the relative-magnitude restriction. The pre-period differential sequence is the twelve-month pre-deal gradient (0.1068 in absolute value, unnormalised), and the post-period estimand is the single twelve-month post-deal gradient (0.7250, SE 0.1995). Confidence sets use a normal approximation with the event-bootstrap standard error at the 95 percent level and impose only the relative-magnitude restriction: the breakdown value is the largest $\bar M$ at which the lower 95 percent bound of the post-period estimate, widened by $\bar M$ times the maximum absolute pre-period gradient, remains above zero.

The breakdown value is the smallest value of $\bar M$ at which the confidence set for the focal estimand contains zero.

### Table D2. Relative-magnitude sensitivity of the state gradient

| Temporal resolution | Breakdown value | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Twelve-month state-gradient calculation | 3.126 | Confidence set first contains zero at $\bar M=3.126$ |
| Quarterly state-gradient calculation | 0 | Pre-period estimates are too noisy to yield an informative positive breakdown value |

*Notes.* The two calculations apply the relative-magnitude framework to estimates at different temporal resolutions. Twelve-month row: pre-period maximum 0.1068, post-period estimate 0.7250 (SE 0.1995). Quarterly row: mean post-deal quarterly gradient 0.1259 (approximate SE 0.0711), pre-period maximum 0.0871. A breakdown value is conditional on the specified class of differential trends and is not a test of whether the identifying restriction is true.

At the twelve-month resolution aligned with the primary state-gradient estimand, the breakdown value is 3.126. Under this calibration, the confidence set first includes zero when the permitted post-treatment differential movement reaches 3.126 times the pre-period benchmark defined by the implemented restriction.

At quarterly frequency, the corresponding calculation produces a breakdown value of zero. This does not indicate that the quarterly point estimates display an adverse pre-trend. Rather, the estimated pre-period movements are too noisy to provide a useful positive scale for the relative-magnitude bound.

The sensitivity result is therefore resolution-dependent in this sample. The twelve-month calculation corresponds to the horizon of the primary estimand, while the quarterly calculation is uninformative because of limited precision. We report both and do not treat either as evidence that the identifying assumption has been validated.

## D.3 Sponsor deployment pressure

Forward-looking sponsor selection remains the main causal limitation of the matched design. ## D.4 Independent confirmation of transaction timing

The monthly event date in the primary analysis comes from PitchBook. Annual shareholder records provide an independent but coarser timing check by identifying the year in which the private equity sponsor first appears among the firm's reported shareholders.

The transaction year can be corroborated using the shareholder records for 62 events. In this subsample, the treated-minus-control change in the share of months with no worker entry is $-0.0645$ [-0.1145, -0.0163], compared with approximately $-0.047$ in the full corresponding sample.

The similarity in direction reduces concern that large transaction-year miscoding is the dominant explanation for the average no-entry pattern. The 62-event sample is small, and the outcome is the no-entry share rather than the primary state gradient in the log hiring rate.

We therefore treat the shareholder comparison as a transaction-year measurement check, not as an independently dated replication of the primary result.

The primary analysis organises post-investment worker-inflow responses by the target’s pre-deal hiring condition. These analyses are supplementary scope checks rather than competing explanations for the primary state gradient. Sample coverage differs across characteristics, so coefficients reported in different panels should not be interpreted as a common-sample ranking.

Sponsor activity is measured using information available before the focal transaction. ## E.1 Transaction form, ownership, and prior sponsor activity

We first examine transaction form and acquired ownership. Adjusted specifications condition on the target’s pre-deal hiring state, log firm size, pre-deal employment growth, firm age, industry, and deal year.

The unadjusted control-minus-growth contrast is 0.1218 log points [-0.0494, 0.2776]. # Appendix F. Supplementary worker earnings and firm outcomes

This appendix provides additional context for the average post-investment changes in worker inflows and employment. The outcomes are not components of the primary state-gradient analysis and are available for different subsets of the matched population. Pension-based earnings have relatively broad coverage, whereas audited financial statements are observed for a smaller and generally larger-firm subsample. We therefore treat these estimates as supplementary matched comparisons rather than mechanism tests or results that automatically generalise to the primary 286-event state-gradient sample.

## F.1 Pensionable earnings

The National Pension records report the contribution base used to calculate pension contributions. Because the contribution base is subject to statutory bounds, it measures pensionable earnings rather than total compensation.

Across 346 events, pensionable earnings per insured worker rise by 0.0096 log points relative to matched controls [0.0039, 0.0154]. The average post-investment increase in employment and worker inflows is therefore accompanied by a small increase in measured pensionable earnings per insured worker.

We also construct a payroll-based earnings measure associated with added employment. The measure is defined as

$$
AE_i
=
\frac{(A_i^{post}-A_i^{pre})/\Delta E_i}{A_i^{pre}/E_i^{pre}}.
$$

The construction uses total reported NPS contribution-base payroll summed over months $-12$ through $-1$ ($A^{pre}$) and months $+1$ through $+12$ ($A^{post}$), the change in window-mean insured employment between the same windows ($\Delta E$), and pre-window average earnings per insured worker ($A^{pre}/E^{pre}$) as the incumbent-earnings normalisation. Observations with $\Delta E_i<2$, including all non-positive changes, are excluded, and the resulting ratio is truncated to $[-3,5]$.

Changes in incumbent earnings enter the construction, so the measure cannot be interpreted as the wage paid to a uniquely identified marginal or newly hired worker. We therefore interpret only its treated-minus-control difference.

## F.3 Sample coverage and continued NPS observation

The audited-firm analyses use a more selected sample than the primary worker-flow analysis. The one-year value-added analysis contains 147 events, compared with 286 events in the primary state-gradient analysis, and audited targets are generally larger. We do not reweight the financial-statement subsample to reproduce the primary matched population. The estimates in Section F.2 therefore describe the targets for which the required audited statements are observed.

Longer-horizon observability requires a separate qualification. A firm can disappear from the National Pension panel because of closure, acquisition, restructuring, administrative re-registration, a sale, or another change in the reporting entity. Continued observation in the NPS panel is consequently not equivalent to firm survival.

We examine whether continued NPS observation varies with the pre-deal hiring state at horizons from twelve to forty-eight months. At the forty-eight-month horizon, targets entering from lower-hiring states are 0.136 less likely to remain observed in the NPS panel [-0.2563, -0.0289].

# Appendix G. Alternative comparison designs and descriptive checks

The analyses in this appendix use comparison groups or classifications that differ materially from the primary matched design. They address four questions: whether the worker-inflow response reverses after sponsor exit, whether firms that receive private equity investment only later provide a useful alternative comparison group, whether similar labour changes occur around non-private-equity ownership transitions, and whether post-deal hiring covaries with realised changes in financial slack.

These exercises do not provide a stronger counterfactual than the primary state-balanced design. Each introduces a separate limitation: sponsor exits are sparse, future-treated controls can be affected by anticipation, non-private-equity ownership changes need not represent comparable interventions, and post-deal cash is itself a treatment-affected variable. We therefore use the analyses to clarify the limits of alternative comparisons rather than as a set of independent replications of the primary estimate.

## G.1 Sponsor exits and reversibility

A labour-market response associated with private equity ownership could weaken or reverse after the sponsor exits. We examine this possibility using annual shareholder records. The private equity holder entering around the original transaction is tracked through subsequent shareholder filings after normalising holder names and grouping identifiable name variants within firms.

Only thirteen sponsor exits satisfy the requirements for this analysis. An event must have an identifiable sponsor departure, an assignable exit period, and at least twelve months of NPS observations following the exit. # Appendix H. Conditional monthly positive-entry hazard

The primary analysis aggregates worker inflows over fixed twelve-month windows. This appendix complements that approach with a monthly discrete-time hazard analysis of whether a firm records at least one NPS worker entry in a month.

The hazard model conditions on duration since the previous positive-entry month. That duration evolves with subsequent worker entries and can itself be affected by private equity investment. Conditioning on it therefore changes the estimand relative to the fixed-window analyses. We interpret the estimates as conditional descriptions of monthly positive-entry incidence rather than alternative causal estimates of the average hiring response or the primary state gradient.

## H.1 Post-investment positive-entry hazard

The hazard analysis contains 48,853 firm-month observations from the 379 baseline matched events. We estimate a discrete-time complementary log-log specification on grouped binomial cells. The model includes event fixed effects and fixed effects for duration since the previous positive-entry month, with standard errors clustered by event.

Duration is defined as the number of consecutive months preceding the current observation without a positive worker entry. The duration categories are $d=1$, $2$, $3$, $4\text{-}5$, $6\text{-}11$, and $\ge 12$ months, with durations capped at 24. A positive-entry month resets the duration clock to one in the following month. At the beginning of the NPS panel, and after any gap in observation, months are excluded until six consecutive observed months have accumulated. The transaction month is excluded: contributing months are event months $-12$ through $-1$ and $+1$ through $+12$, with the post indicator equal to one for the latter.

| Specification | Hazard ratio | 95% CI |
|---|---:|---:|
| Post-investment positive worker entry, treated relative to matched controls | 1.1593 | [1.0834, 1.2409] |

*Notes.* The outcome equals one if a firm records at least one NPS worker entry during the month. Estimates come from a discrete-time complementary log-log model fitted to grouped binomial cells. The grouped likelihood is equivalent to the corresponding firm-month likelihood. Duration since the previous positive-entry month enters through fixed effects for the categories $d=1$, $2$, $3$, $4\text{-}5$, $6\text{-}11$, and $\ge 12$ months. The model includes event fixed effects and clusters standard errors by event. The sample contains 379 matched events and 48,853 firm-month observations. The treated-post hazard ratio is 1.159 [1.083, 1.241]. Thus, conditional on the variables included in the model, the post-investment hazard of recording a positive worker-entry month is 15.9 percent higher for treated firms relative to their matched controls.

The direction is consistent with the positive average hiring-rate change in Table 2, but the magnitudes are not directly comparable. The fixed-window outcome aggregates the number of entries over twelve months. The hazard specification instead models whether any entry occurs in a month conditional on the duration since the preceding positive-entry month. It therefore discards the number of entries within a positive-entry month while conditioning on a variable that evolves with prior post-investment entries.

The hazard estimate provides supplementary evidence that positive worker-entry incidence increases after investment. It is not a second estimate of the magnitude of the twelve-month worker-inflow response.

## H.2 Pre-deal hiring state and the monthly positive-entry hazard

Extending the monthly hazard specification to the pre-deal hiring state uses a different source of variation from the primary event-level state-gradient estimator. The primary design first constructs a twelve-month treated-minus-control response and then relates that response to the target’s continuous pre-deal state. The hazard model instead estimates a conditional monthly positive-entry probability under event fixed effects and duration controls.

The state variable used in the hazard analysis is the treated target's continuous pre-deal state, assigned to every firm-month within that event's matched set (controls carry the target's state, not their own), entered without rescaling. This distinction matters because state balancing in the matching procedure and state variation used in the hazard interaction are not the same operation.

Under conventional matching, which does not exact-match on the state tercile, the treated-by-post-by-continuous-state interaction has a hazard ratio of 1.204 [1.0205, 1.4205]. The specification uses 308 events with the required earlier state measurement, 3,919 grouped cells, and 41,303 firm-month observations.

Under state-balanced matching, the corresponding continuous-state interaction has a hazard ratio of 1.507 [0.8710, 2.6087] across 1,199 grouped cells. The point estimate remains above one, but the interval is wide and includes one.

A tercile-based state interaction cannot be separately estimated in the state-balanced event-fixed-effect specification because the state-tercile indicator used in the exact matching structure is collinear with the event fixed effects.

|---|---:|---:|

The state-balanced continuous specification uses 308 events, 1,199 grouped cells, and 38,652 firm-month observations. “Conventional matching” refers to the design that does not include the hiring-state tercile in the exact matching cell. The state variable used in the continuous interaction is the treated target's pre-deal state $S_i=-\log(1+N_i^{state}/E_i^{state})$ over months $-24$ through $-13$, unscaled and common to all firm-months within the matched event. All models include event fixed effects and duration-category fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by event. The state-tercile interaction is not separately estimable in the state-balanced specification because the corresponding tercile indicator is collinear with the event fixed effects.

The monthly hazard interaction and the primary event-level state gradient are not estimates of the same parameter. The primary estimator relates the treated-minus-control fixed-window response to the target’s pre-deal state after constructing controls balanced on that state. The hazard model instead conditions on duration since the previous positive-entry month and estimates a monthly incidence relationship under event fixed effects.

Under conventional matching, the continuous-state hazard interaction is positive and more precisely estimated. Under state-balanced matching, the point estimate remains positive but is imprecise. The state-balanced hazard result therefore neither provides a precise independent replication of the primary gradient nor constitutes a falsification of it.

The appropriate interpretation is narrower. The monthly hazard analysis provides supplementary evidence on the incidence of positive worker-entry months. The primary evidence on state-dependent adjustment remains the fixed-window matched gradient, for which the state-balanced comparison and untreated reference distribution are constructed directly around the event-level estimand.
