## Abstract

Private-equity employment outcomes vary substantially across target firms. Existing work has mainly organised this heterogeneity around the transaction or investor, including deal type, financing conditions, and sponsor characteristics. We ask whether the target’s own labour-adjustment state before investment also organises its subsequent response. We link private-equity investments to monthly Korean National Pension records. The baseline matched design contains 379 investment events, of which 286 enter the primary state-balanced heterogeneity analysis. Firms with lower pre-deal hiring intensity increase hiring substantially more after investment. The gradient in the change in the log hiring rate is 0.7101 log points [0.3187, 1.1254], and an interquartile shift toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.2005-log-point larger response [0.0900, 0.3177]. Untreated low-hiring firms also rebound, but 1,246 pseudo-events processed through the identical procedure generate a mean gradient of only 0.101; the target estimate lies 3.96 standard deviations above this empirical null (upper-tail placebo p = 0.0005). Balancing the pre-deal hiring state is consequential because the same state predicts untreated hiring dynamics: without that balance, the control path varies systematically with the treated firm’s state and contributes to the estimated heterogeneity. The hiring gradient is also present in raw hire counts. Low-hiring targets experience a larger increase in worker churn, while the employment gradient is negative and the separation gradient is not separately detected. The state-dependent response is therefore concentrated in gross worker-flow activity rather than differential net employment growth. The findings place the target’s pre-existing labour-adjustment state alongside deal and sponsor characteristics as an organising dimension of private-equity employment heterogeneity. The internal organisational channel remains unidentified, and sponsor private information about future growth limits a causal interpretation.

# 1. Introduction

Private-equity investment is followed by sharply different employment paths across target firms. Existing evidence has organised much of this variation around characteristics of the transaction or investor. Employment and other real-side outcomes differ by transaction type, financing conditions, and the identity of the sponsoring private-equity group (Davis et al. 2025). These dimensions describe the deal or the sponsor. They leave open whether the target’s own condition before investment helps explain which firms adjust most afterwards.

We examine one such condition: the target’s pre-deal hiring state. A firm that has hired few workers for a sustained period enters the transaction with a different recent history of labour adjustment from a firm that has been hiring intensively. If that state matters, two otherwise comparable targets need not exhibit similar post-investment hiring responses even when they receive the same broad form of capital and ownership intervention. The relevant empirical question is therefore not only whether hiring changes after private-equity investment, but whether the magnitude of that change varies systematically with how actively the target was hiring beforehand.

The same state creates a counterfactual problem. Firms with low hiring intensity may resume hiring without private-equity investment. If the extent of that rebound varies with the moderator used to organise treatment-response heterogeneity, conventional matching on industry, size, prior employment growth, and age is not sufficient. The controls assigned to low- and high-hiring targets may themselves have systematically different subsequent hiring paths. A gradient in treated-minus-control outcomes can then combine heterogeneity among treated firms with heterogeneity in the counterfactual. Pre-deal hiring state must consequently be evaluated on both sides of the comparison: as a characteristic of the target and as a predictor of untreated outcome dynamics.

We study these questions by linking private-equity investments identified in PitchBook to monthly workplace records from Korea’s National Pension system. The administrative records contain monthly worker entries, separations, and insured employment, allowing us to measure a target’s earlier hiring state separately from the period used to construct the outcome. The baseline matched design contains 379 investment events. The primary heterogeneity analysis uses 286 events for which the pre-deal state, complete outcome windows, and state-balanced controls are available. We measure the state over months -24 to -13 relative to the transaction and the outcome as the change in the log hiring rate from months -12 to -1 to months +1 to +12, relative to matched untreated firms. Higher values of the state correspond to lower pre-deal hiring intensity.

The post-investment hiring response varies strongly along this dimension. The state gradient is 0.7101 log points, with a treated-firm-clustered 95 percent bootstrap interval of [0.3187, 1.1254]. An interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.2005-log-point larger response [0.0900, 0.3177], or approximately 22 percent on the corresponding rate scale. This quantity is a cross-target gradient in the post-investment response, not an average treatment effect. It asks how much more hiring changes among targets that entered the transaction from a lower-hiring state than among otherwise comparable targets that had been hiring more actively.

Mean reversion makes zero the wrong benchmark for this gradient. Untreated firms with low hiring intensity also tend to rebound. We therefore construct the empirical null from untreated firms rather than impose a zero-gradient null. Each eligible untreated firm is assigned a pseudo-event and processed through the same state construction, matching, adjustment, winsorisation, and estimation procedure as the targets. The 1,246 resulting pseudo-events generate a mean state gradient of 0.101 with a standard deviation of 0.1538. The target gradient exceeds that mean by 0.6091 log points and lies 3.96 standard deviations above the placebo distribution (upper-tail placebo p = 0.0005). Thus, untreated low-hiring firms do exhibit a rebound, but the state dependence observed after private-equity investment is considerably larger than the rebound generated by comparable untreated firms.

How the controls are constructed is material to that comparison. On the 286 events common to both specifications, the conventional design, which matches on industry, size, pre-deal employment growth, and age, produces a state gradient of 0.4698. Adding a tercile of the pre-deal hiring state to the matching cell raises the gradient to 0.7101. The change comes from the counterfactual rather than from a different pattern among the treated firms. In the corresponding unwinsorised decomposition, the treated-side gradient is essentially unchanged across the two matching procedures, whereas the control-group gradient with respect to the treated firm’s state falls from 0.295 to 0.0056. The state-balanced design therefore removes a systematic relationship between the moderator and the assigned control path rather than generating the result by selecting a different treated response.

This point matters beyond the particular matching specification used here. A pre-treatment characteristic can organise both treatment-response heterogeneity and untreated outcome dynamics. When it does, balance on conventional firm characteristics does not ensure a valid comparison along the dimension used to estimate heterogeneity. In our setting, explicitly balancing the hiring state leaves the treated-side relationship intact while largely eliminating the control-side dependence on that state. The implication is conditional: when a proposed moderator also predicts untreated paths, a matched heterogeneity design needs to account for that dependence when constructing the counterfactual.

The state gradient is not an artefact of scaling hiring by employment. Replacing the hiring rate with the raw log count of hires yields a gradient of 0.6315. Conditioning additionally on the change in log employment gives 0.6957. The cross-target pattern therefore comes primarily from worker inflows rather than from movements in the employment denominator.

Nor does a larger hiring response imply larger net employment growth. Worker churn has a positive state gradient of 0.3816, whereas the gradient in log employment is -0.0580. Targets that had been hiring less before the deal subsequently exhibit larger increases in hiring and gross worker-flow activity, but they do not exhibit correspondingly larger net employment gains. The estimated separation gradient is positive at 0.1788, but it is not separately detected relative to its placebo distribution. We therefore do not identify a separation or worker-reallocation channel. The evidence supports the narrower conclusion that the state-dependent response is pronounced in worker inflows and broader gross-flow activity while differential net employment growth moves in the opposite direction.

This distinction extends the employment evidence in the private-equity literature. Davis et al. (2014) show that substantial gross job creation and destruction can coexist with relatively modest net employment effects after private-equity buyouts. Davis et al. (2025) show that real-side outcomes also vary materially with buyout type, market conditions, and the sponsoring group. Our evidence adds the target’s pre-existing labour-adjustment state to this account of heterogeneity. More importantly, that state separates two margins that average employment outcomes can obscure: which targets exhibit the largest worker-flow response and whether those same targets experience the largest net employment gains. In our data, they do not.

Observed transaction characteristics provide a useful comparison rather than an alternative explanation of the result. In the 180-event sample for which the relevant deal characteristics are jointly observed, those characteristics have little detectable in-sample explanatory power for the hiring response, whereas the pre-deal state has stronger in-sample explanatory content. Neither set of variables performs well out of sample, and the difference in out-of-sample fit is not detected. We therefore use pre-deal hiring intensity as an organising variable for the realised heterogeneity in this sample, not as a validated forecasting rule for future targets, investment returns, or net job creation.

The evidence is also more informative about the incidence of the response than about its internal organisational source. Private-equity ownership can coincide with changes in financing capacity, governance, managerial incentives, operational practices, recruiting capacity, and product demand (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009; Boucly et al. 2011; Gompers et al. 2016). The administrative records do not distinguish among these channels. A low pre-deal hiring state may mark a period of limited labour adjustment, but the estimates do not structurally identify an inaction band or show which intervention moves a firm out of that state.

Selection on sponsor private information is the more consequential limitation. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring firm because it anticipates a contract, demand expansion, financing event, management change, or other development that is not visible in our data. Untreated firms with the same observed hiring history need not share that information. The pseudo-event results rule out ordinary untreated rebound of the observed magnitude as a sufficient explanation, and the non-overlapping pre-deal analysis does not reveal a positive state gradient comparable to the post-investment estimate. Diagnostics based on observable pre-trends and ex ante fund deployment pressure also run against simple versions of a selection account. None of these exercises observes the sponsor’s target-specific information set, however. We therefore interpret the estimates as a state-dependent post-investment response within the matched population rather than as evidence that private-equity ownership itself causes the entire gradient.

The population boundary is similarly important. Matching retains targets for which comparable untreated firms can be constructed, and these targets are concentrated toward the smaller end of the Korean private-equity target population. The estimates therefore describe the firms represented in that matched support. They need not extend to larger public companies, transactions outside Korea, or settings in which employment registration, labour-market institutions, or the composition of private-equity investment differs materially. Within that boundary, the evidence yields a specific conclusion: the target’s pre-existing hiring state helps organise where the post-investment worker-flow response is concentrated, and that heterogeneity is not captured by net employment growth alone.

# 2. Related literature and empirical motivation

## 2.1 Private equity, employment, and heterogeneous adjustment

Research on private equity shows that employment responses following investment are neither uniform across firms nor fully captured by changes in the employment stock. Davis et al. (2014) document relatively modest changes in net employment alongside substantially larger gross job creation and destruction around private equity buyouts. More recent evidence shows that real-side outcomes also vary across transaction types, financing conditions, and private equity sponsors (Davis et al. 2025). These findings shift attention from a single average employment response toward the sources and margins of heterogeneity in post-investment adjustment.

Evidence from other institutional settings reinforces the difficulty of characterising private equity with a single labour-market effect. Studies report post-transaction growth among French LBO targets, job polarisation following Swedish buyouts, and worker-level risks in German transactions (Boucly et al. 2011; Olsson and Tåg 2017; Antoni et al. 2019). Evidence from Asia includes employment outcomes following Japanese buyouts and operating outcomes around Korean restructuring transactions (Blind and Lottanti von Mandach 2021; Koo 2016). The Korean setting offers a different empirical opportunity: monthly administrative records allow us to observe the hiring condition of predominantly private targets before investment and worker flows afterwards.

The distinction between net employment and gross adjustment is especially relevant for our outcomes. In the labour literature, gross job creation and destruction can be substantial even when net employment changes little (Dunne et al. 1989; Davis and Haltiwanger 1992). Our data measure a related but distinct margin: worker entries and exits rather than the creation and destruction of jobs. A firm can replace workers or otherwise change workforce composition while leaving its employment stock largely unchanged. Monthly worker-flow records therefore allow us to distinguish variation in net employment growth from variation in the movement of workers into and out of the firm.

Private equity research has also broadened the set of worker outcomes under study, including career paths, workplace safety, pay dispersion, and the roles of labour-market power and implicit employment contracts (Agrawal and Tambe 2016; Cohn et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2026; Herkenhoff et al. 2025). Our question is different. We study cross-target variation in post-investment labour adjustment and ask whether that variation is organised by an observable condition of the target before the transaction.

Existing evidence on heterogeneous private equity outcomes has largely organised variation around the transaction or the investor. Deal type captures differences in ownership and control, financing conditions describe the environment in which a transaction occurs, and sponsor characteristics distinguish the organisations undertaking the investment (Davis et al. 2025). The target’s recent hiring condition is conceptually different. Two firms can receive investments with similar ownership structures from similar sponsors while entering the transaction with very different recent hiring histories. One may already be adding workers regularly; another may have undertaken little hiring over the preceding year. If the scope or need for subsequent labour adjustment depends on that starting condition, transaction- and sponsor-level characteristics alone need not describe where the largest post-investment responses occur.

This target-side perspective changes the heterogeneity question in two ways. First, it concerns the incidence of post-investment adjustment: where, across targets, the largest changes occur. Second, the characteristic used to organise heterogeneous responses may also predict what would have happened without the transaction. If untreated hiring dynamics vary systematically with the same pre-deal state, a comparison group balanced only on conventional firm characteristics can still generate a counterfactual that varies with the moderator. The empirical design must therefore consider pre-deal hiring not only as a source of heterogeneity among targets, but also as a characteristic of the untreated comparison path.

## 2.2 Interpreting the pre-deal hiring state

The pre-deal hiring state is intended to summarise a recent condition of the target rather than to measure a single underlying friction. Low hiring can arise for economically different reasons. A firm may face limited access to finance, may have postponed expansion, may have undertaken little organisational adjustment, or may simply have experienced weak recent demand. The same observed hiring state can therefore correspond to different internal conditions, and our empirical measure does not distinguish among them.

Financing constraints provide one possible interpretation. Boucly et al. (2011) report stronger post-buyout growth among French firms classified as more likely to have been financially constrained and interpret the pattern as consistent with buyouts relaxing those constraints. More broadly, access to capital is one channel through which private equity can affect investment and growth, particularly among private firms for which external financing may be costly or limited (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009). If low prior hiring partly reflects financing that constrained expansion, private equity investment could be followed by a larger hiring response when that constraint is relaxed. This interpretation is plausible but not unique, and pre-deal hiring intensity is not a direct measure of financial constraints.

Private equity may also affect firms through governance, managerial incentives, operational intervention, and growth strategies (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009; Gompers et al. 2016). A target that has undertaken relatively little labour adjustment before investment may have greater scope for subsequent recruiting, replacement, expansion, or reorganisation. Conversely, a target already hiring intensively may have less room for a further proportional increase over the same horizon. These possibilities give economic content to recent hiring history without assigning it to a particular organisational channel. Our data observe worker flows and employment, but not the managerial decisions or internal frictions that generate them.

Models of labour demand with non-convex adjustment costs provide a related interpretation. When adjustment involves fixed or nonlinear costs, firms need not change employment continuously as underlying conditions evolve; periods of limited activity can be followed by larger adjustments once the gains become sufficiently large (Cooper et al. 2015). Persistent low hiring over the state window can be viewed through this lens as a period of limited labour adjustment. We use this framework only to motivate why recent hiring history may contain information about subsequent adjustment. The empirical state does not identify a structural inaction region, and worker inflows are not a direct measure of the employment-adjustment friction in such models.

A simpler explanation requires no change in financing, governance, or organisational policy. Low-hiring firms may simply revert toward their usual level of activity. A firm can temporarily stop recruiting, contract, or pass through an unusually quiet hiring period and then resume hiring around the transaction date. Because the main outcome is a proportional change in hiring, low initial activity can also leave greater scope for subsequent increases. A positive association between low prior hiring and later hiring is therefore not informative by itself. The relevant question is whether the state gradient following private equity investment is unusually large relative to the gradient generated by firms that enter comparable observable states without receiving private equity investment.

This distinction motivates two features of the empirical design. First, we construct untreated pseudo-events and process them through the same state definition, matching procedure, outcome transformation, and estimation method used for private equity targets. The resulting distribution provides an empirical benchmark for state-dependent rebound rather than imposing a zero-gradient null. Second, the primary matching design balances the pre-deal hiring state itself. This step matters because a moderator that predicts untreated dynamics can otherwise enter the estimated heterogeneity gradient through the comparison-group outcome. Section 4 describes both procedures in detail.

Recent hiring history can also be distinguished from a short-lived pause immediately before investment. If the gradient mainly reflects a temporary interruption in recruiting, it should be closely related to recent employment contraction or to the length of the hiring pause already in progress at closing. If the state instead captures a broader recent condition, hiring activity over the preceding state window should be more informative than the duration of the current pause, while a state measured much further in the past should be less closely related to the subsequent response. We use these comparisons to assess what the state summarises, rather than to identify a unique organisational mechanism.

These comparisons leave a more consequential selection problem unresolved. A private equity sponsor may invest in a low-hiring firm because it has information about future demand, a new contract, financing for expansion, a management change, or another development that is not visible in our administrative and financial records. Untreated firms with similar observed hiring histories need not share that information. Pre-deal dynamics can reveal whether treated firms were already diverging along observable outcomes, but they cannot rule out information that remains latent until investment occurs. The empirical analysis therefore distinguishes unusually large post-investment responses from observable state-dependent rebound; it does not identify the internal channel or eliminate selection on sponsor private information.

We therefore use the pre-deal hiring state to organise three empirical questions. Does post-investment hiring vary with recent target hiring intensity by more than would be expected from untreated firms entering comparable states? Is the relationship more consistent with a broader recent hiring condition than with a short pause immediately before the transaction? And does the resulting heterogeneity appear in net employment growth or principally in worker inflows and related worker-flow activity? The remainder of the paper uses the monthly administrative data and matched design to address these questions while maintaining the distinction between the observed state, its possible economic interpretations, and the causal effect of private equity investment. `feedback.md` `feedback.md`

# 3. Institutional setting and data

## 3.1 National Pension records and worker-flow measurement

We use monthly administrative records from Korea’s National Pension Scheme (NPS) to measure worker flows and insured employment. For each reporting workplace, the records identify entries and exits of NPS-insured workers and the number of insured employees. Their monthly frequency is central to the empirical design: it allows us to measure the target’s hiring history well before the transaction and to observe worker inflows, exits, and employment on a common time scale around the investment.

The primary state and outcome windows do not overlap. We measure the target’s pre-deal hiring state over months -24 through -13. The pre-investment component of the primary outcome is measured over months -12 through -1, and the corresponding post-investment component over months +1 through +12. Separating the state and baseline outcome windows prevents the same worker entries from mechanically defining both the moderator and the subsequent change in hiring. Monthly records also allow us to distinguish worker-flow activity from changes in the employment stock: worker entries and exits can be substantial even when net employment changes relatively little.

NPS reporting is administered at the workplace (*saeopjang*) level, whereas private equity transactions are linked using the business registration number associated with the pension filing. A business may therefore appear through more than one registered workplace. We aggregate the pension records to the business-registration-number level and treat that identifier as the empirical firm unit. We separately track changes in the number of registered workplaces around the transaction because administrative consolidation or reorganisation could otherwise generate apparent worker entries or exits without an equivalent change in the underlying workforce.

Throughout the empirical analysis, an NPS entry is interpreted as an insured-worker inflow. For expositional convenience, we use “hire” and “hiring” when referring to the corresponding empirical measures, but the records do not independently identify a recruiting decision. An entry records the acquisition of NPS-insured status at the reporting workplace and can occasionally arise from employee re-registration, payroll consolidation, or another administrative change. This qualification is particularly relevant around changes in ownership. Section 5.2 and Online Appendix C therefore examine the employment-flow accounting identity, changes in the number of registered workplaces, and an inflow measure reconstructed from employment changes and recorded separations.

The pension records cover insured workers rather than every individual who supplies labour to the firm. Workers outside NPS coverage, informal employment, hours worked, job titles, recruiting expenditures, and the managerial decision that generated a particular entry or exit are not observed. We require at least five insured employees under the sample rules described below to reduce the influence of very small workplaces. The data are therefore well suited to measuring insured-worker inflows, exits, and employment at high frequency, but not to identifying the organisational mechanism that produces those flows.

The NPS records also report the contribution base used to calculate pension contributions. Because that base is subject to statutory minimum and maximum amounts, it measures pensionable earnings rather than total compensation. We use this variable only in the supplementary worker-earnings analysis in Section 7.3.

## 3.2 Private equity investments, sample construction, and supplementary data

We identify private equity investments in Korean companies from PitchBook and link the transaction records to the NPS panel using business registration numbers. Additional links recovered from firm names are manually reviewed before inclusion. The treatment definition follows the private equity classification used to construct the transaction sample and is not limited to leveraged buyouts or changes in control. It includes minority growth investments as well as transactions involving control. We therefore use “private equity investment” for the treated events generally and reserve “buyout” or “control transaction” for the corresponding subsets. Of the 206 sponsor identifiers in the treated universe, 62 are associated with two or more observed deals, accounting for 61.8 percent of events with an identified sponsor.

The resulting setting differs from the large public-to-private buyouts that feature prominently in the U.S. literature. Targets in our matched sample are predominantly smaller private firms, with median insured employment at the transaction of approximately 79 workers. The sample includes both minority and control investments and both domestic and foreign sponsors. These features allow us to study a broader set of private-company investments, but they also bound the population to which the estimates apply. The results should not be read as estimates for large public-to-private buyouts or for private equity transactions in institutional settings not represented by the Korean sample.

The NPS panel runs monthly from November 2015 through May 2026. The baseline matching universe contains transaction dates from December 2016 through June 2026 because matching uses pre-investment information and does not itself require a complete post-investment outcome window. Events near the end of the panel can therefore enter the matching universe even when they cannot contribute to a post-investment outcome estimate. The primary state-gradient sample ends earlier, in May 2025, because it requires both the earlier state window and a complete twelve-month post-investment outcome window.

We begin with 752 identified private equity investment events. Of these, 168 cannot be linked to an NPS record, 120 do not have the required pre-investment history, 75 have fewer than five insured employees at the transaction, and 10 have no eligible untreated firm in the baseline matching cell. These restrictions leave 379 events in the baseline matched design. The average-effect analysis and the state-gradient analysis then impose different outcome and state-availability requirements and should be viewed as separate branches of this baseline sample rather than as sequential stages of attrition.

The average-effect analysis contains 367 events with the required pre- and post-investment outcome observations for the target and at least one matched control. The primary state-gradient design additionally requires a complete earlier hiring-state measure, an eligible control in the state-balanced matching cell, and the observations required for the log hiring-rate outcome. These restrictions yield 286 target events. The comparison design that does not balance the hiring state contains 301 events; comparisons between the two matching procedures are made on their common 286-event sample. The 379 baseline events correspond to distinct business registration numbers, so the treated-event sample does not contain repeated observations of the same target firm.

Sample selection affects the scope of the estimates. Targets entering the matched design have median insured employment of about 80 workers, compared with about 136 among identified targets that do not enter it. The history, matching, and outcome requirements therefore shift the estimation sample toward the smaller end of the observed target population. Our estimates are most informative about private equity investments in smaller Korean private firms for which sufficiently complete pension histories and comparable untreated firms are available.

We supplement the monthly NPS records with annual audited financial statements and shareholder records. The financial statements provide assets, revenue, cash, debt, interest expense, and operating income, but statutory audit coverage produces a smaller and generally larger-firm subsample. We therefore use these data for supplementary analyses rather than condition inclusion in the primary worker-flow sample on financial-statement availability. Annual shareholder records provide holder identity and ownership stakes and are used to corroborate the year in which the sponsor stake first appears and to construct ownership measures. # 4. Research design

## 4.1 Matching and the pre-deal hiring state

For the baseline matched comparisons, we match each private equity target to five untreated firms observed in the same transaction month. Potential controls must first fall in the same cell defined by two-digit industry and bins for firm size, pre-deal employment growth, and firm age. The control pool excludes all 752 firms identified as private equity targets. A target without an eligible untreated firm in its cell is excluded rather than matched outside the region of observed support.

Firm size is measured using mean insured employment over months -6 through -1 and grouped at 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 250 employees. Pre-deal employment growth compares employment over months -6 through -1 with months -18 through -13 and is grouped using -10 and +10 percent cut-offs. Firm age is measured from the firm’s first NPS enrolment and grouped at five and fifteen years. Within each cell, we select the five nearest controls using log pre-deal employment and pre-deal employment growth. The neighbour distance is $((\Delta\log\text{employment})/0.9)^2+((\Delta\text{growth})/0.35)^2$, with growth clipped to $[-1,2]$ before differencing.

The matching procedure is designed to restrict the comparison to firms entering the event with similar observed characteristics. It does not make private equity investment conditionally random. The design follows the general logic of matching on pre-treatment covariates and overlap (Abadie and Imbens 2006; Stuart 2010; Iacus et al. 2012), while the identifying requirement in this setting remains that the matched untreated firms provide a suitable counterfactual path conditional on the variables used in the design.

The primary heterogeneity analysis additionally balances the target’s pre-deal hiring condition. We measure the state over months -24 through -13 as

$$
S_i=-\log\left(1+\frac{N_i^{state}}{E_i^{state}}\right),
$$

where $N_i^{state}$ is the number of NPS worker entries over the state window and $E_i^{state}$ is mean insured employment over the same twelve state-window months (months -24 through -13). Higher values of $S_i$ indicate lower pre-deal hiring intensity. Because the state window ends at month -13, no worker entry used to construct $S_i$ also enters the months -12 through -1 baseline component of the primary outcome.

For the primary gradient analysis, we add a tercile of $S_i$ to the matching cell and rematch the eligible targets. The continuous value of $S_i$, rather than the tercile indicator, is used to estimate the response gradient. The tercile restriction is used only to construct controls entering the event from a more comparable region of prior hiring activity. This state-balanced procedure yields 286 target events.

Balancing the state matters because the paper studies heterogeneity across target states rather than only an average treated-control difference. Suppose the untreated change in hiring depends on a firm’s pre-deal state. If low-hiring untreated firms tend to rebound more strongly than high-hiring untreated firms, a control selected without regard to $S_i$ can generate a comparison path that itself varies with the treated firm’s state. A slope in the treated-minus-control outcome can then combine variation in the treated response with variation in the constructed counterfactual.

This problem is distinct from ordinary mean imbalance. Controls can be well balanced with targets in industry, size, recent employment growth, and age while their subsequent outcomes still vary systematically with the moderator used to estimate heterogeneity. In that case, a gradient in the matched difference need not describe only how the treated outcome varies across states; part of it can arise because the assigned counterfactual changes along the same dimension.

The state-balanced design addresses this observable source of counterfactual heterogeneity by requiring closer alignment in the moderator before outcomes are compared. Section 5.2 examines whether the resulting difference between the two matching designs comes from the treated or control component. State balancing does not address unobserved information that distinguishes targets from untreated firms, nor should it be interpreted as establishing random assignment conditional on the observed state.

## 4.2 Outcome and estimand

The primary outcome measures the change in worker inflows over non-overlapping twelve-month windows before and after the transaction. For firm $i$ and window $w$, define the hiring rate as

$$
HR_{iw}=\frac{N_{iw}}{\bar E_{iw}},
$$

where $N_{iw}$ is the total number of NPS worker entries in the window and $\bar E_{iw}$ is mean insured employment over the same months. The rate is not annualised. The pre-investment window covers months -12 through -1 and the post-investment window covers months +1 through +12.

For target $i$, let $C(i)$ denote its matched controls. We construct the event-level matched response as

$$
D_i=
\left[\log HR_{i,post}-\log HR_{i,pre}\right]
-
\frac{1}{|C(i)|}
\sum_{j\in C(i)}
\left[\log HR_{j,post}-\log HR_{j,pre}\right].
$$

The primary estimand is the slope of $D_i$ with respect to the target’s continuous pre-deal hiring state $S_i$. A positive coefficient therefore means that targets entering the transaction with lower prior hiring intensity exhibit a larger post-investment increase in the log hiring rate relative to their matched controls. This parameter describes heterogeneity in the matched post-investment response across target states; it is not the average post-investment response.

For the primary state-gradient analysis, both twelve-month outcome windows must be completely observed. A firm-window is excluded when the required monthly observations are missing, mean insured employment falls below the applicable five-worker threshold, or no worker inflow is recorded and the logarithm is therefore undefined. The positive-hiring requirement removes three treated firm-windows. Online Appendix B reports inverse-hyperbolic-sine and $\log(1+HR)$ specifications that retain zero-hiring windows.

The separate average-effect benchmark in Table 2 uses a different availability rule, requiring at least six observed months in each pre- and post-investment window for the target and an eligible control. The 367-event average-effect sample and the complete-window sample used for the primary state gradient therefore correspond to different estimands and observation requirements.

The log transformation puts proportional changes in worker inflows on a comparable scale across firms with different initial hiring rates. It does not, however, eliminate all mechanical concerns. Insured employment appears in the denominator and can itself change after the transaction. We therefore repeat the state comparison using the raw log number of worker entries and a specification that additionally conditions on the change in log employment. These analyses distinguish a gradient in worker inflows from one generated primarily by the employment denominator.

We adjust the gradient for log pre-deal employment, pre-deal employment growth, firm age, and one-digit industry, all measured before the transaction. Let $\widetilde D_i$ and $\widetilde S_i$ denote the residuals obtained after partialling these variables out of the event-level response and the state, respectively. The primary coefficient is estimated from

$$
\widetilde D_i=\beta \widetilde S_i+\varepsilon_i.
$$

Thus, $\beta$ describes variation in the matched response associated with the component of the pre-deal hiring state not accounted for by the included target characteristics. Residualisation does not make the state exogenous and does not remove differences in unobserved target characteristics.

The primary specification winsorises the event-level outcome at the 5th and 95th percentiles of the treated sample. The resulting absolute cut-offs are held fixed when the same procedure is applied to untreated pseudo-events. Online Appendix B reports the unwinsorised estimate, 1/99 and 10/90 cut-offs, transformations that retain zero-hiring windows, and alternative matching and estimation choices.

The coefficient $\beta$ is a within-sample heterogeneity parameter for the matched population. It is distinct from the average post-investment response reported in Section 5.1 and from a forecasting coefficient for new transactions. Section 7.2 separately evaluates out-of-sample prediction.

## 4.3 Untreated reference distribution and statistical inference

A conventional zero-centred reference distribution is not appropriate for the primary state gradient. Firms with unusually low prior hiring can subsequently increase hiring even without a private equity transaction. Mean reversion, temporary hiring pauses, and other state-dependent untreated dynamics can therefore generate a positive relationship between the pre-deal state and later hiring. We estimate the magnitude of that background relationship directly from untreated firms.

Each eligible matched control is treated in turn as a pseudo-event. The pseudo-event is assigned the corresponding event date, matched to five untreated controls under the same design, and passed through the same state construction, outcome transformation, covariate adjustment, and winsorisation procedure used for the private equity targets. This procedure produces 1,246 untreated pseudo-events. We repeatedly draw samples from this pool at the size of the treated sample and re-estimate the state gradient, generating 2,000 draws from an untreated reference distribution.

The reference distribution is not forced to be centred at zero. It therefore incorporates state-dependent rebound, functional-form effects, and features of the estimation pipeline that also arise when the focal private equity event is absent. The relevant comparison is not whether the target gradient differs from zero, but whether its magnitude is unusual relative to gradients produced by untreated firms entering comparable observable states and processed through the same procedure.

We do not interpret this exercise as a randomisation test. Private equity investment is not randomly assigned, and the pseudo-events do not reproduce an experimental assignment mechanism. The reference distribution instead calibrates how unusual the observed state gradient is relative to untreated dynamics generated by the same empirical procedure. It does not correct for unobserved information that systematically distinguishes private equity targets from untreated firms.

For the primary specification, we report a two-sided empirical tail probability of 0.0005, together with the target-event bootstrap confidence interval. The reported probability equals $(1+\#\{b:|g_b-\bar g|\ge|g^{obs}-\bar g|\})/(B+1)$, where $g_b$ are the $B=2000$ simulated gradients, $\bar g$ is their mean, ties count toward the numerator, and the add-one correction bounds the probability below by $1/(B+1)=0.0005$. Because the untreated reference distribution need not be symmetric, the two-sided probability is calculated directly from the simulated draws rather than by mechanically doubling the directional upper-tail probability. The originally specified upper-tail comparison is retained as a supplementary benchmark.

Confidence intervals are obtained by resampling matched target-control event sets so that each target and its controls move together. The treated targets are distinct firms. In the primary 286-event sample, the matched sets use 1,144 distinct control firms; approximately 95 percent of these controls appear in only one event and none appears in more than three. Online Appendix D reports inference that clusters on control firms and a specification restricting each control to a single event. Because those alternatives do not indicate greater uncertainty than the target-event bootstrap, we retain the target-event interval as the primary confidence interval.

We distinguish statistical imprecision from evidence that an outcome is economically small. For secondary outcomes, a confidence interval containing zero is described as “not detected” unless the entire interval lies within a prespecified range of practical equivalence. This distinction is important for outcomes such as separations, where an imprecise estimate does not establish the absence of an economically meaningful response.

The pre-deal hiring-state gradient in the log hiring rate is the primary heterogeneity analysis. Average post-investment employment and hiring changes provide benchmark quantities, and worker-flow contrasts are used to locate the margin of adjustment. Monthly hiring allocation, transaction and sponsor characteristics, worker earnings, and audited financial outcomes are secondary analyses. Alternative state definitions, window choices, matching specifications, and treatment-timing designs are treated as sensitivity or exploratory analyses and are reported primarily in the Online Appendix.

A causal interpretation of the gradient requires an additional assumption that the matching and pseudo-event procedures cannot verify: conditional on the observed pre-treatment variables and hiring state, the matched controls must approximate the state-specific hiring path that the target would have experienced without private equity investment. State balancing addresses the observable problem that untreated hiring dynamics vary with the moderator. It does not address selection on information observed by the sponsor but absent from the administrative and transaction data. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring target because it anticipates future demand, financing, a new contract, managerial change, or another development that comparable untreated firms do not share. The gradient in log employment is -0.0580, compared with an untreated reference mean of 0.0796; its standardized distance from that reference is -2.60 and the two-sided empirical $p$-value is 0.007. Thus, moving toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity is associated with a larger worker-inflow response but a smaller net-employment response. This negative gradient does not imply that employment falls on average: Section 5.1 shows an average matched employment change of approximately 9 percent. The two estimates describe different objects-the average matched change and the cross-target gradient in that change.

Worker churn also moves positively with the state. The gradient in the log of worker entries plus separations per employee is 0.3816, with a standardized distance of 3.08 from its untreated reference distribution. Because worker entries are themselves a component of churn, however, this result is not an independent test of a separation response. The separation-rate gradient is positive at 0.1788 but is imprecise relative to its untreated distribution (standardized distance 1.17; two-sided $p=0.2349$). We therefore do not infer a distinct increase in worker exits or a specific replacement mechanism.

Within-event outcome contrasts reinforce the distinction between worker inflows and employment stocks. On the same events, the hiring-minus-employment gradient is 0.7536, compared with an untreated reference mean of 0.0232, while the churn-minus-employment gradient is 0.4539 (Table 4, Panel C). Post-investment heterogeneity across pre-deal hiring states is therefore most clearly concentrated in worker inflows and does not translate into correspondingly larger net-employment gains. The evidence is consistent with greater gross worker-flow activity among low-hiring targets, but the imprecise separation gradient prevents a stronger claim about two-sided worker reallocation.

# 6. Validity, interpretation, and remaining selection

## 6.1 Untreated rebound and pre-deal hiring dynamics

The matched design addresses observable differences in the comparison group, but it does not by itself establish that private equity investment causes the larger hiring response among low-hiring targets. Two observable alternatives can nevertheless be examined directly. First, firms with low prior hiring may rebound even without private equity investment. Second, eventual private equity targets may already be moving onto different hiring paths before the transaction.

The untreated pseudo-event analysis addresses the first concern. As reported in Section 5.2, the untreated reference distribution has a mean state gradient of 0.101, compared with 0.7101 among private equity targets. Low-hiring untreated firms therefore exhibit some subsequent rebound, but its magnitude is much smaller than the target gradient. Ordinary state-dependent recovery is unlikely to account for the observed target gradient on its own. This comparison does not address forces specific to firms subsequently selected for private equity investment.

We next examine whether a similar state-dependent divergence was already visible among eventual targets before investment. Constructing this diagnostic involves a trade-off because the primary state is measured over months -24 through -13. Our first specification avoids overlap between the state and the earlier outcome by moving the state window back to months -36 through -25. Relative to the corresponding untreated pseudo-event benchmark, the resulting pre-deal excess gradient is -0.0777 [-0.4394, 0.0854] across 250 events (Table 5). The upper endpoint is well below the post-deal gradient of 0.7101, providing little evidence of a positive pre-deal divergence of comparable magnitude. This comparison is mechanically cleaner, but it changes the moderator relative to the primary specification.

A complementary specification retains the usual state window but is less clean because the state and the earlier outcome share observations. Among the 283 events available for this comparison, the pre-deal gradient is 0.1068 [-0.1955, 0.4351], whereas the post-deal gradient on the same events is 0.7250 [0.3259, 1.1081]. The pre-deal point estimate is substantially smaller than the post-deal estimate, although its confidence interval remains compatible with a meaningful positive pre-existing relationship. We therefore treat the two specifications as complementary diagnostics with different limitations rather than as evidence that the pre-deal gradient is exactly zero.

Quarterly estimates reported in Online Appendix D do not reveal a persistent positive pre-deal gradient, but they are imprecise. The mean treated pre-deal gradient across the four quarters is -0.012 [-0.163, 0.131]. These estimates therefore provide no clear indication of an existing positive ordering before investment, while leaving moderate differential dynamics within the range supported by the data.

A twelve-month relative-magnitude sensitivity calibration following Rambachan and Roth (2023) yields a breakdown value of 3.126. The corresponding quarterly calculation is uninformative because the pre-period gradients are too noisy to provide a stable scale for the bound. We therefore treat the exercise as a sensitivity calibration rather than as validation of the identifying assumption. Full calculations are reported in Online Appendix D.

## 6.2 What does the pre-deal hiring state capture?

The state gradient can reflect different aspects of the firm’s recent history. Online Appendix B distinguishes a broader low-hiring condition from a temporary pause immediately before the transaction. Controlling for pre-deal employment growth leaves a positive state coefficient of 0.410 [0.078, 0.753]. The historical share of months with no hiring is also positively related to the response, with an estimate of 0.559 [0.241, 0.885], whereas the length of the hiring pause already in progress at closing has a much smaller estimate of 0.040 [-0.016, 0.107]. When the historical no-hire measure and pause duration enter jointly, the corresponding estimates are 0.536 and 0.032. These comparisons suggest that the primary state summarises hiring activity over a broader recent period rather than merely the duration of a pause at the transaction date.

The state should not, however, be interpreted as a distinct structural firm characteristic. Size, industry, pre-deal employment growth, age, and seasonality jointly account for 63.9 percent of its variation. After removing this observable component, the residualised-state estimate remains positive at 0.243 but is imprecise [-0.221, 0.723]. The residualised specification therefore does not establish a separate state association orthogonal to these observable characteristics.

Hiring activity measured further in the past is also less informative about the subsequent response. Moving the state window to months -36 through -25 produces a gradient of 0.268 against a specification-specific untreated mean of 0.141; the standardized distance is 0.92 and the upper-tail placebo $p$-value is 0.1789. Together with the comparisons based on the hiring pause, this attenuation is more consistent with the state summarising a relatively recent hiring condition than a time-invariant firm type. It does not establish what underlying organisational or financial condition generates that recent history. `Online Appendix 0831.pdf` `Online Appendix 0831.pdf`

## 6.3 Forward-looking sponsor selection and the causal boundary

The preceding diagnostics provide much less leverage against a different form of selection: private information held by the sponsor about future firm conditions. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring firm because it expects demand to increase, anticipates a large customer contract, has arranged financing for expansion, or plans a management change that will be followed by additional hiring. Matched untreated firms can share the same observed hiring history without sharing this information.

This failure case need not generate an observable pre-deal trend. If the sponsor’s information affects realised hiring before investment, the diagnostics in Table 5 can constrain the resulting divergence. If the information remains latent in observed employment outcomes until the transaction occurs, however, the absence of a visible pre-deal gradient does not eliminate the selection channel. The pre-deal evidence therefore narrows the class of selection stories but cannot distinguish a response caused by private equity investment from forward-looking selection on signals that become observable only afterwards.

Online Appendices D and G examine several related comparisons. A fund-age proxy for deployment pressure does not support the narrow prediction that greater sponsor timing discretion is associated with a larger post-deal hiring response. Because fund age is neither randomly assigned nor a direct measure of the sponsor’s information set, however, this comparison does not materially relax the private-information concern. Independent shareholder records provide a limited check on transaction-year measurement, while alternative comparisons based on not-yet-treated targets, sponsor exits, and non-private-equity ownership changes are either imprecise or economically different from the primary matched comparison. We use these exercises to clarify what can be learned from the available data rather than as alternative designs that solve the selection problem.

Observable financial condition offers another, more limited way to examine possible interpretations of the state. Across the audited pre-deal statements, none of the standardised financial measures predicts the hiring response: cash over assets gives +0.0255 [−0.0650, 0.1152] (n = 186), leverage −0.0045 [−0.1027, 0.0875] (n = 186), interest coverage +0.0489 [−0.0420, 0.0891] (n = 170), and profitability −0.0055 [−0.0911, 0.1018] (n = 184), per standard deviation of each measure. On the same audited sample, the pre-deal hiring state remains predictive, at +0.1087 [0.0229, 0.1964] per standard deviation (n = 169). The observable financial variables therefore do not reproduce the information in the hiring state, although the audited subsample is smaller and the intervals are wide. These measures are imperfect proxies for financing constraints, and the audited subsample is smaller and generally contains larger firms than the primary pension-based sample. They therefore cannot isolate financing constraints from governance, product demand, managerial capacity, recruiting access, or other possible channels. Post-investment financial variables provide still less leverage for mechanism identification because they may themselves respond to the investment.

The evidence consequently supports a bounded interpretation of the primary result. Ordinary state-dependent rebound among untreated firms is too small to account for the target gradient on its own, and the available pre-deal diagnostics do not reveal a positive divergence of comparable magnitude before investment. These findings make ordinary mean reversion and continuation of an already visible hiring trend less plausible as complete explanations. They do not eliminate forward-looking selection on information unavailable in the pension, transaction, and financial data.

We therefore interpret the primary estimate as an unusually large post-investment hiring response among low-hiring targets relative to observably comparable untreated firms, rather than as an unconditional causal effect of private equity investment. The data establish where the post-investment response is concentrated more clearly than why it occurs. `feedback.md` `Online Appendix 0831.pdf` `Online Appendix 0831.pdf`

# 7. Additional evidence and scope checks

## 7.1 Post-investment hiring dynamics

The average matched hiring difference appears soon after the transaction and extends beyond the twelve-month horizon used for the main outcome. In the quarterly specification, the treated-minus-control hiring-rate difference is positive in the first post-investment quarter, with a 95 percent interval of [0.0032, 0.0250], and the subsequent point estimates remain positive through the tenth quarter (Figure 2). The first four post-investment quarterly estimates sum to approximately 0.048, close to the 0.0485 twelve-month matched change reported in Table 2. We use these quarterly estimates to describe the timing of the average post-investment difference rather than as the primary state-heterogeneity estimand.

Targets also record worker inflows in a broader set of months after investment. This change need not represent a separate change in hiring timing. A firm that hires more workers over a fixed horizon will mechanically tend to have fewer months with zero worker entries even if the allocation of those entries across months is otherwise unchanged. We therefore compare the observed monthly pattern with a volume-preserving benchmark that holds each firm-window’s realised number of worker entries, observed months, and monthly employment exposure fixed while reallocating the entries across months.

The observed share of months with no worker entries declines by 0.0466 [-0.0675, -0.0270]. The volume-preserving benchmark implies a decline of 0.0518 [-0.0699, -0.0326], leaving an excess change of 0.0052 [-0.0082, 0.0189]. The entire interval for the excess lies within a ±0.046 comparison range calibrated to the magnitude of the observed change. Under this benchmark, the increase in monthly hiring participation is therefore largely accounted for by the increase in realised hiring volume rather than by a comparably large residual reallocation of hiring across months. Online Appendix A reports the corresponding results for the number and duration of no-entry months, the concentration of worker entries across months, and a longer thirty-six-month window.

The benchmark is descriptive rather than structural. It does not model firm-specific seasonality, month-specific demand shocks, or persistent differences in the tendency to concentrate hiring in particular months. We therefore interpret the result narrowly: higher hiring volume accounts for most of the observed change in monthly participation under the stated allocation benchmark. The exercise does not establish that private equity investment leaves the timing of recruiting decisions unchanged.

# 8. Conclusion

Using monthly administrative worker-flow records linked to private equity investments in Korea, we examine whether post-investment hiring responses vary with the target’s own hiring condition before the transaction. They do. In the primary state-balanced sample, an interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity is associated with a 0.2005-log-point larger treated-minus-control change in the hiring rate. Low-hiring untreated firms also rebound, but the corresponding state gradients generated by untreated pseudo-events are much smaller. The main empirical result is therefore not simply that low-hiring firms subsequently hire more, but that the post-investment gradient among private equity targets is unusually large relative to firms entering comparable observable states without a private equity event.

The heterogeneity is clearest in worker inflows. The same positive state relationship appears when worker entries are measured without employment in the denominator, whereas net employment varies in the opposite direction along the same pre-deal state dimension. A churn measure also rises more among low-hiring targets, but the separation gradient is too imprecise to establish a distinct exit or replacement margin. We therefore draw the narrower conclusion that the largest state-dependent differences occur in worker inflows and do not translate into correspondingly larger net-employment gains.

These findings shift attention from characteristics of the transaction and sponsor to the condition of the target entering private equity investment. Existing work has documented important heterogeneity across transaction types, financing environments, and sponsors. Our evidence shows that recent target hiring history provides a distinct way to organise post-investment labour adjustment within the matched population. The matching exercise adds a related design consideration. When the pre-treatment variable used to organise heterogeneous responses also predicts untreated outcome dynamics, conventional covariate balance need not produce a comparison path that is neutral with respect to that moderator. In our setting, explicitly balancing the hiring state largely removes this control-side dependence. The setting therefore illustrates a broader design consideration: when the variable used to organise heterogeneous responses also predicts untreated outcome paths, the construction of the comparison group should account explicitly for that dependence.

The design also places clear limits on interpretation. Private equity investment is not randomly assigned, and the matched comparisons cannot eliminate selection on information available to sponsors but absent from the administrative and financial data. The pre-deal and untreated-firm analyses weigh against ordinary state-dependent rebound and continuation of a comparably large hiring divergence that was already visible before investment, but they cannot exclude forward-looking selection on latent demand, financing, contracts, or managerial changes. Nor do the data identify a unique organisational channel. Finally, the matched population is concentrated among smaller Korean private firms and includes both growth investments and transactions involving control. Within these boundaries, recent hiring history distinguishes where post-investment worker inflows are most pronounced: targets entering the transaction after a period of low hiring exhibit much larger inflow responses without correspondingly larger net-employment gains. `feedback.md` `feedback.md`

# Appendix A. Monthly hiring allocation and the volume benchmark

The main text shows that private equity targets record worker entries in a broader set of months after investment. Some of this change follows mechanically from the increase in total worker inflows. Holding the length of the observation window fixed, a firm that records more entries will generally have fewer months with no entries even if the allocation of those entries across months otherwise remains unchanged. This appendix compares the observed monthly pattern with a benchmark that holds realised worker-entry volume fixed. It then decomposes the average hiring-rate change using a formulation that is algebraically consistent with the primary hiring-rate measure.

## A.1 Construction of the volume benchmark

For each firm-window, let $N$ denote the realised number of worker entries and $E_j$ insured employment in month $j$. Define

$$
w_j=\frac{E_j}{\sum_{k}E_k},
$$

so that $w_j$ is month $j$’s share of total insured-employment exposure over the window. Our primary benchmark holds $N$, the observed months, and monthly employment exposure fixed and reallocates the $N$ entries across months with probabilities $w_j$.

Under this employment-exposure-weighted allocation, the expected number of months with no worker entry is

$$
\sum_j(1-w_j)^N.
$$

As a sensitivity benchmark, we also allocate worker entries with equal probability across the observed months. For a complete twelve-month window, the corresponding expected number of no-entry months is

$$
12\left(\frac{11}{12}\right)^N.
$$

For monthly-allocation measures without a closed-form expectation-the longest spell without a worker entry, the Herfindahl index of entries across months, and the share of entries occurring in the two busiest months-we simulate the multinomial allocation 100 times for each firm-window. The simulation uses the same realised $N$ and employment-exposure probabilities $w_j$ in each firm-window. The benchmark value for each measure is the mean across the simulated allocations. Each firm-window uses 100 multinomial draws under a fixed seed (42), so the benchmark values are exactly reproducible.

For any monthly-allocation measure $M$, define the residual, or excess, change as

$$
\Delta M^{excess}
=
\Delta M^{observed}
-
\Delta M^{benchmark}.
$$

The excess therefore measures the part of the treated-minus-control change in monthly allocation that is not implied by realised worker-entry volume under the stated allocation benchmark. It is not a regression coefficient conditional on realised volume.

The benchmark holds fixed realised worker-entry volume, the set of observed months, and monthly insured-employment exposure. It does not model firm-specific seasonality, month-specific demand shocks, or persistent differences in the tendency to concentrate worker entries in particular months. The exercise is therefore a benchmark for the mechanical relation between total entry volume and monthly participation or concentration, rather than a structural model of hiring timing.

Before the transaction, treated firms average 91.9 worker entries per twelve-month window. They have 3.23 months with no worker entry, compared with 2.58 under the primary benchmark. Their longest no-entry spell averages 1.97 months, compared with 1.53. The Herfindahl index of monthly worker entries is 0.203 in the data and 0.163 under the allocation benchmark; the corresponding shares of entries in the two busiest months are 0.468 and 0.378. Thus, before the transaction, worker entries are more concentrated across months than predicted by the employment-exposure-weighted allocation benchmark.

## A.3 Decomposition of the average hiring increase

The primary twelve-month hiring rate is defined as total worker entries divided by mean insured employment over the same window. For a twelve-month window,

$$
HR
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12}N_j}{\bar E}
=
\frac{12\sum_{j=1}^{12}N_j}
{\sum_{j=1}^{12}E_j}.
$$

Let $A=\{j:N_j>0\}$ denote the set of months with positive worker entries. Define

$$
q
=
\frac{\sum_{j\in A}E_j}
{\sum_{j=1}^{12}E_j},
$$

the share of total insured-employment exposure occurring in months with positive worker entries, and

$$
i_E
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{12}N_j}
{\sum_{j\in A}E_j},
$$

worker-entry intensity per unit of employment exposure in active months. These definitions give the exact identity

$$
HR=12q i_E.
$$

Accordingly,

$$
\log HR
=
\log 12+\log q+\log i_E,
$$

and the pre-to-post change satisfies

$$
\Delta\log HR
=
\Delta\log q+\Delta\log i_E.
$$

The same additivity holds after taking treated-minus-control changes. Unlike a decomposition based on the unweighted share of active months and the simple mean of monthly entry rates, this exposure-based decomposition remains algebraically consistent with the hiring-rate measure used in the main analysis when employment varies across months.

# Appendix C. Validation of administrative worker inflows and flow outcomes

The main analysis interprets monthly National Pension Scheme entries as administrative worker inflows. Two features of the records warrant closer examination. =
(E_{it}-E_{i,t-1})

\frac{|d_{it}|}
{[\text{placeholder: exact employment denominator used in the code}]}.
$$

## D.2 Relative-magnitude sensitivity of the state gradient

We apply the relative-magnitude sensitivity framework of Rambachan and Roth (2023) to assess how conclusions change when differential post-investment trends are permitted to depart from the pattern observed before investment.

For the twelve-month implementation, we map the pre- and post-investment state gradients estimated on the same 283 events (pre-deal gradient 0.1068; post-deal gradient 0.7250, SE 0.1995) into the relative-magnitude restriction by treating the absolute pre-deal gradient as the maximal observed pre-period differential sequence and the single twelve-month post-deal gradient as the post-period estimand. The sensitivity parameter $\bar M$ bounds the absolute post-period differential trend relative to the maximum absolute pre-period gradient. Confidence sets use a normal approximation with the event-bootstrap standard error: the breakdown value is the largest $\bar M$ at which the lower 95 percent bound of the post-period estimate, widened by $\bar M$ times the pre-period maximum, remains above zero. The reported breakdown value is the smallest $\bar M$ at which the corresponding confidence set contains zero.

## D.3 Sponsor deployment pressure

Selection on sponsor private information remains a limitation of the matched design. One possible version of this concern operates through transaction timing: sponsors with more discretion may wait until favourable expansion prospects become apparent to them even when those prospects are not observed in the data.

We examine this possibility using the age of the sponsor’s most recent private equity fund at the transaction date as a proxy for deployment pressure. Fund age is measured from the most recent fund close month; when an exact close month is unavailable, we use the midpoint of the reported vintage year. High deployment pressure is defined as the top tercile of fund age, with a cutoff of 2.42 years.

In a discrete-time complementary log-log specification, the treated-by-post-by-high-deployment-pressure interaction has a hazard ratio of 1.2881 [1.0535, 1.5750]. The model uses 379 event clusters, 4,686 grouped cells, and 48,853 firm-month observations. It includes event fixed effects and duration-bucket fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by event.

The point estimate indicates a larger conditional post-deal positive-entry response among transactions associated with older sponsor funds. It therefore runs opposite to the narrow prediction that sponsors with greater timing discretion should display a larger hiring response.

This comparison does not identify exogenous variation in investment timing. Fund age may covary with sponsor strategy, target composition, fundraising conditions, or other unobserved features of the transaction, and it is not a direct measure of the sponsor’s information set. In addition, the hazard model conditions on duration since the previous worker entry. Because this duration evolves as post-transaction entries occur, it is itself affected by subsequent hiring history. The interaction is therefore a conditional descriptive comparison rather than an alternative causal estimate of the state gradient. The deployment-pressure result does not remove selection on sponsor private information.

## D.4 Independent confirmation of transaction timing

The primary event dates come from PitchBook. Annual shareholder records provide an independent timing check by identifying the year in which the private equity sponsor’s ownership stake first appears.

## F.1 Pensionable earnings

The National Pension records report the contribution base used to calculate pension contributions. Because the contribution base is subject to a statutory ceiling, it measures pensionable earnings rather than total compensation.

Across 346 events, pensionable earnings per insured worker rise by 0.0096 log points relative to matched controls [0.0039, 0.0154]. The average post-investment employment expansion is therefore accompanied by a small increase in measured pensionable earnings per insured worker.

We also construct a payroll-based added-employment earnings measure. The measure relates the change in payroll to the change in employment and normalises the resulting quantity by incumbent earnings:

$$
AE_i
=
\frac{(A_i^{post}-A_i^{pre})/\Delta E_i}{A_i^{pre}/E_i^{pre}},
$$

The construction uses total reported NPS contribution-base payroll summed over months -12 through -1 ($A^{pre}$) and months +1 through +12 ($A^{post}$), the change in window-mean insured employment between the same two windows ($\Delta E$), and pre-window average earnings per insured worker ($A^{pre}/E^{pre}$) as the incumbent-earnings denominator. Observations with $\Delta E_i<2$ (including all non-positive changes) are excluded, which prevents denominator explosion; the resulting ratio is truncated to $[-3,5]$. No other winsorisation is applied.

Changes in incumbent earnings enter the numerator of this measure. Its level therefore cannot be interpreted as the earnings paid to a uniquely identified marginal worker. We interpret only the treated-minus-control difference in the constructed index.

# Appendix G. Alternative comparison designs and descriptive checks

The analyses in this appendix use comparison groups or classifications that differ materially from the primary matched design. They address four questions: whether the worker-inflow response reverses after sponsor exit, whether not-yet-treated private equity targets provide a useful alternative control group, whether similar labour changes occur around non-private-equity ownership transitions, and whether post-deal hiring covaries with realised changes in financial slack. None of these exercises provides a stronger counterfactual than the primary state-balanced design. Their purpose is to show what can, and cannot, be learned from alternative comparisons available in the data.

# Appendix H. Conditional monthly positive-entry hazard

The primary analysis measures worker inflows over fixed twelve-month windows. This appendix complements that approach with a monthly discrete-time hazard specification for the probability that a firm records at least one NPS worker entry in a month.

The model conditions on the duration since the previous positive-entry month. That duration evolves as subsequent worker entries occur and can itself be affected by the private equity transaction. Conditioning on duration therefore changes the estimand relative to the fixed-window comparisons. We interpret the hazard specifications as descriptive analyses of monthly worker-entry incidence, not as alternative estimates of either the twelve-month average response or the primary state gradient.

## H.1 Post-investment positive-entry hazard

The hazard sample contains 48,853 firm-month observations from the 379 baseline matched events. We estimate a discrete-time complementary log-log model using grouped binomial cells. The specification includes event fixed effects and fixed effects for duration categories defined by the number of consecutive months since the previous positive worker-entry month. Standard errors are clustered by event.

Duration categories are $d=1$, $d=2$, $d=3$, $d=4\text{-}5$, $d=6\text{-}11$, and $d\ge 12$ months since the last positive-entry month, with durations capped at 24 months. The duration clock resets to one in the month after a positive-entry month. Observations at the beginning of the NPS panel (or after gaps in observation) are excluded until six consecutive observed months have accumulated, and the transaction month itself is excluded; contributing months are event months -12 through -1 and +1 through +12.

*Notes.* The outcome equals one when a firm records at least one NPS worker entry in a month. The hazard estimate therefore provides supplementary evidence on monthly worker-entry incidence rather than a second estimate of the magnitude of the twelve-month response.

## H.2 The pre-deal hiring-state interaction in the monthly hazard model

Extending the monthly hazard specification to the pre-deal hiring state uses a different source of variation from the event-level matched-difference estimator. The primary estimator first constructs a treated-minus-control fixed-window response and then relates that response to the target’s continuous pre-deal state. By contrast, the hazard model estimates a conditional monthly positive-entry probability under event fixed effects.

The state variable attached to each firm-month in the hazard specification is the treated target's pre-deal state, assigned to every firm in that event's matched set (treated target and controls alike). This definition should be read separately from the matching restriction itself.

Under conventional matching, which does not exact-match on the hiring-state tercile, the treated-by-post-by-continuous-state interaction has a hazard ratio of 1.204 [1.0205, 1.4205]. The specification uses 308 events with the required earlier state measurement, 3,919 grouped cells, and 41,303 firm-month observations.

Under state-balanced matching, the corresponding continuous-state interaction is 1.507 [0.8710, 2.6087]. This specification uses 308 events, 1,199 grouped cells, and 38,652 firm-month observations. The point estimate remains positive, but the confidence interval is wide and includes one.
