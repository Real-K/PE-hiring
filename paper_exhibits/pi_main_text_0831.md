Private Equity and State-Dependent Hiring
Daewoong Kang
ABSTRACT
Research on private equity and employment has largely explained variation in post-transaction outcomes through deal characteristics, financing conditions, or the sponsoring group. We examine whether the target’s own hiring history before investment is also associated with variation in its subsequent response. Using monthly Korean social-security records and matched private equity targets, we find that firms with lower pre-deal hiring intensity exhibit substantially larger post-deal increases in hiring. An interquartile shift toward lower prior hiring is associated with a 0.20-log-point larger response. The state gradient lies far above the distribution generated by untreated pseudo-events processed through the same matching and estimation procedure. Because untreated hiring dynamics also vary with the pre-deal state, matching controls on prior hiring activity materially changes the counterfactual; without this balance, state-dependent movements among the controls contribute to the estimated gradient. The same pattern appears in raw hire counts. Low-hiring targets also experience larger increases in worker churn, while net employment moves in the opposite direction. This combination points to a response concentrated in gross worker flows rather than differential net expansion. In the common sample, observed transaction characteristics explain little response variation, and most of the change in monthly hiring frequency follows from higher hiring volume. Post-investment labour adjustment is strongest among firms with low pre-deal hiring intensity, while the internal organisational channel and selection on sponsor private information remain unresolved.

1. Introduction 
Employment responses following private equity investment vary sharply across target firms. Existing work has organised much of this heterogeneity around characteristics of the transaction and the investor. Buyout type, credit conditions at closing, and the sponsoring private equity group are associated with materially different real-side outcomes (Davis et al. 2025). Less is known about variation associated with the condition of the target itself when the investment occurs. A firm that has been hiring steadily enters the transaction from a different labour-adjustment state than one that has added few workers over the preceding year. We ask whether post-investment hiring responses differ systematically with that pre-deal hiring history.
This question poses a counterfactual problem as well as a substantive one. Low-hiring firms can rebound even without private equity investment. If that tendency also varies with the state used to define treatment-response heterogeneity, matching firms only on conventional characteristics can leave untreated outcome paths systematically related to the moderator. The resulting gradient in treated-minus-control outcomes can then reflect both differences among treated firms and differences in what their controls would have done. Pre-deal hiring history must therefore be evaluated not only as a source of heterogeneity among targets, but also as a characteristic of the counterfactual.
We study this question using monthly Korean National Pension records linked to private equity investments identified in PitchBook. The records provide monthly worker entries, exits, and insured employment, allowing the target’s earlier hiring condition to be separated from the period used to measure its subsequent response. The baseline matched design contains 379 investment events. The primary heterogeneity analysis uses 286 events for which the pre-deal hiring state, outcome windows, and state-balanced controls are all available. We measure the state over months −24 to −13 and compare the change in the log hiring rate from months −12 to −1 with the corresponding change over months +1 to +12. Higher values of the state indicate lower pre-deal hiring intensity.
Firms that enter the transaction with lower hiring intensity subsequently increase hiring much more than firms that had already been hiring actively. The primary state gradient is 0.7101 log points, with a treated-firm-clustered 95 percent bootstrap interval of [0.3187, 1.1254]. An interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.2005-log-point larger response [0.0900, 0.3177], approximately 22 percent on the corresponding rate scale. The magnitude is distinct from the average post-investment response: employment rises by approximately 9 percent across matched targets on average, whereas the state gradient describes how the response differs across targets with different pre-deal hiring histories.
Mean reversion cannot be assessed against a zero benchmark. We therefore apply the same matching, state construction, adjustment, and estimation procedure to untreated firms. The resulting 1,246 pseudo-events generate a state-gradient distribution with mean 0.101 and standard deviation 0.1538. The target gradient exceeds that mean by 0.6091 log points and lies 3.96 standard deviations above it (upper-tail placebo . Untreated low-hiring firms do rebound, but the magnitude of that rebound is far smaller than the gradient observed following private equity investment.
Balancing the pre-deal state changes the counterfactual rather than the pattern among treated firms. On the 286 events common to the two designs, matching on industry, size, pre-deal employment growth, and age produces a gradient of 0.4698; adding the hiring-state tercile raises it to 0.7101. In the corresponding unwinsorised decomposition, the treated-side gradient is unchanged across the two matching procedures, while the control-group gradient with respect to the treated firm’s state falls from 0.295 to 0.0056. The difference between the two estimates is thus generated by the control path. Without state balancing, part of the untreated rebound associated with the moderator enters the estimated treatment-response gradient.
The result is also present when hiring is measured without employment in the denominator. The gradient in the raw log count of hires is 0.6315, and it is 0.6957 after conditioning on the change in log employment. Worker churn rises more strongly among low-hiring targets as well, with a state gradient of 0.3816. Net employment moves differently: its gradient is −0.0580, even though employment rises on average across matched targets. Firms with the largest hiring responses therefore do not have the largest net employment gains along the same state dimension. The separation gradient is positive at 0.1788 but imprecise relative to its placebo distribution, so the data do not isolate worker exits as a separate channel. The cross-target response is most clearly visible in worker inflows and gross worker-flow activity rather than differential net employment growth.
This distinction connects the result to two strands of the private equity literature. Davis et al. (2014) show that private equity buyouts can involve substantial gross job creation and destruction even when net employment effects are modest. More recent work documents large differences in real-side effects across buyout types, credit conditions, and private equity sponsors (Davis et al. 2025). Our evidence adds a different dimension to that heterogeneity: the labour-adjustment condition of the target before the transaction. In the 180-event sample with complete transaction characteristics, the pre-deal hiring state has clearer within-sample explanatory content than control transfer, acquired stake, and sponsor deal count. The corresponding out-of-sample estimates are weak for both sets of variables, however, so the state should not be read as a validated forecasting rule for future targets.
The matching analysis adds a related design implication. A pre-treatment characteristic used to organise heterogeneous responses can also organise untreated outcome dynamics. When it does, conventional balance on firm size, industry, growth, or age does not ensure that the counterfactual is comparable along the dimension used to estimate heterogeneity. In our setting, explicitly balancing the hiring state removes almost all of the control-side dependence on the treated state while leaving the treated-side gradient unchanged. The broader implication is conditional rather than mechanical: when a proposed moderator also predicts untreated paths, a matched heterogeneity design needs to account for that dependence in constructing the comparison group.
The data identify the form and incidence of the response more clearly than its internal organisational source. Prior work gives several reasons why private equity investment may alter firm behaviour, including relief of financing constraints, changes in governance and managerial incentives, and operational intervention (Boucly et al. 2011; Kaplan and Strömberg 2009; Gompers et al. 2016). Any of these channels could contribute to the hiring response observed here, as could changes in product demand or recruiting capacity. The administrative data do not distinguish among them, and pre-deal hiring intensity should not be interpreted as a proxy for one specific mechanism.
Selection on sponsor private information poses a separate and more consequential limit to causal interpretation. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring firm because it anticipates future demand, a new contract, financing for expansion, a management change, or another development that is not visible in our administrative and financial records. Untreated firms with similar observed hiring histories need not share that information. The non-overlapping pre-deal analysis does not show a positive divergence comparable in magnitude to the post-investment gradient, which weighs against the simplest account in which targets were already on visibly different hiring paths. It cannot rule out information that remains latent in observed outcomes until the investment occurs. The matched design therefore supports an unusually large post-investment hiring response among low-hiring targets relative to observably comparable untreated firms; stronger causal attribution remains conditional on the absence of sufficiently large selection on unobserved sponsor information.
The scope of the estimates is also specific. The matched Korean sample is concentrated among smaller private firms and includes minority growth investments as well as transactions involving control. The results need not transport to large public-to-private buyouts or to private equity markets operating under different institutional conditions. Within that population, recent hiring history distinguishes where post-investment workforce adjustment is most pronounced, and the accompanying worker-flow evidence shows that the difference appears primarily in gross labour adjustment rather than larger net employment growth.
2. Related literature and empirical motivation
2.1 Employment responses to private equity
Research on private equity has established that changes in employment are only one part of the adjustment that follows a buyout. Davis et al. (2014) find relatively modest effects on net employment alongside much larger changes in gross job creation and destruction, with private equity targets reallocating activity more intensively than comparable firms. Subsequent evidence also shows substantial heterogeneity in these real-side outcomes. Davis et al. (2025) document large differences across buyout types, credit conditions around the transaction, and the private equity groups sponsoring the deals. The employment consequences of private equity therefore depend not only on whether a transaction occurs but also on the circumstances under which it occurs.
Evidence outside the United States reinforces this heterogeneity. French LBO targets grow more rapidly after acquisition (Boucly et al. 2011), Swedish buyouts are associated with job polarization (Olsson and Tåg 2017), and German evidence points to greater human-capital risk for workers (Antoni et al. 2019). Evidence from Asia remains more limited. Blind and Lottanti von Mandach (2021) study employment following Japanese buyouts, while Koo (2016) examines operating outcomes in Korean restructuring transactions. Together, these studies make it difficult to characterise the labour-market consequences of private equity with a single average employment effect.
This distinction between net and gross adjustment also has a longer history in the labour literature. Gross job creation and destruction are large even when aggregate or firm-level net employment changes are modest because expansion in some units can occur alongside contraction in others (Dunne et al. 1989; Davis and Haltiwanger 1992). The distinction matters especially in the present setting. A firm can replace workers, reorganise jobs, or increase both entries and exits without producing a correspondingly large change in employment. A hiring response therefore need not be a net job-creation response. Observing entries, exits, and employment at a monthly frequency allows us to ask not only whether employment changes after private equity investment, but also whether differences across targets appear primarily in net expansion or in gross worker-flow activity.
A separate private equity literature examines how ownership and governance may alter firm behaviour. Kaplan and Strömberg (2009) review the roles of leverage, managerial incentives, governance, and active ownership in the economics of leveraged buyouts. Survey evidence from private equity investors likewise indicates that sponsors expect to affect portfolio companies through operational and governance changes and often place substantial weight on growth rather than cost reduction alone (Gompers et al. 2016). Empirical work has examined operational changes in particular industries (Bernstein and Sheen 2016), capital structure and operating performance after leveraged buyouts (Cohn et al. 2014), and sources of value creation in private-company buyouts (Cohn et al. 2022). Other studies focus directly on workers, including career paths (Agrawal and Tambe 2016), workplace safety (Cohn et al. 2021), pay dispersion (Fang et al. 2026), and the interaction of private equity ownership with monopsony and implicit employment contracts (Herkenhoff et al. 2025).
Much of the empirical heterogeneity in this literature is organised around the transaction or the investor. Deal type distinguishes, for example, control transactions from growth investments; financing conditions describe the environment in which the transaction is funded; and sponsor characteristics capture differences in the organisations making the investments. Fund-level returns also differ across private equity partnerships and have historically displayed persistence (Kaplan and Schoar 2005), although deal-level evidence indicates that performance persistence has weakened as the industry has matured (Braun et al. 2017). These are economically important sources of variation, and Davis et al. (2025) show that transaction and sponsor characteristics are associated with large differences in real-side outcomes.
The target’s labour-adjustment condition before the transaction is conceptually different. It describes neither how the deal is financed nor who undertakes it, but the state in which the operating firm enters the transaction. Two firms can receive investments with similar ownership structures from sponsors with similar experience while arriving at the deal with very different hiring histories. One may already be expanding its workforce regularly; another may have hired little over the preceding year. If the scope or need for post-investment adjustment depends on that starting condition, transaction characteristics alone need not capture where the largest labour response occurs.
This distinction changes the empirical question. The issue is not simply whether one more observable characteristic predicts heterogeneous private equity outcomes. If pre-deal hiring history organises the response, average employment effects combine firms entering the transaction from economically different labour-adjustment states. The resulting heterogeneity concerns the **incidence** of post-investment adjustment: which targets change their labour inputs most strongly, conditional on entering private equity investment. It also bears on the construction of the counterfactual. A pre-treatment state that predicts the response may itself predict what would have happened without the transaction. In that case, the same state that defines the heterogeneity comparison must be considered when constructing untreated comparison paths. Section 4 develops this design issue directly.
2.2 Why pre-deal hiring may matter
One interpretation of low pre-deal hiring comes from the literature on financing constraints. Boucly et al. (2011) find stronger post-buyout growth among French firms that were more likely to have been financially constrained and interpret the pattern as consistent with buyouts relaxing those constraints. Their classification is based on pre-buyout ownership, industry dependence on external finance, and firm size rather than contemporaneous accounting ratios. More broadly, access to capital is one of the channels through which private equity ownership may alter investment and growth, particularly at private targets for which external financing may be more costly or limited (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009).
This argument provides one possible explanation for a larger hiring response among firms that had previously hired little. If limited hiring reflects a binding financing constraint, an investment that relaxes the constraint could permit the firm to undertake previously deferred expansion. We examine this possibility using pre-deal cash, leverage, interest coverage, and profitability. Those variables do not produce a comparably clear gradient in the audited subsample, but the comparison is necessarily limited. Financial statements are available for fewer and generally larger targets, and our accounting measures are not the constraint measures used by Boucly et al. (2011). The matching design also already conditions on firm size, while private ownership is nearly universal in our sample. The analysis therefore does not constitute a direct test of their financing-constraint result and cannot rule out financing as an internal channel.
Low hiring can also arise for reasons unrelated to external finance. Private equity investors report using changes in governance, managerial incentives, operating practices, and growth strategies to affect portfolio companies (Kaplan and Strömberg 2009; Gompers et al. 2016). A target that has undertaken little labour adjustment before the transaction may have greater scope for organisational change, recruiting, expansion, or replacement afterwards. Conversely, a target already hiring intensively may have less room for a further proportional increase. Our data do not observe these internal decisions closely enough to distinguish among them. Pre-deal hiring intensity is therefore a state variable in the empirical analysis, not a proxy for an identified mechanism.
Models of labour demand with non-convex adjustment costs provide a related way to interpret that state. When adjustment entails fixed or non-linear costs, firms need not change employment continuously as underlying conditions evolve. They can remain relatively inactive over a range of conditions and undertake larger adjustments once the gains from doing so become sufficiently large (Cooper et al. 2015). Persistent low hiring over the state window can be viewed through this lens as a period of limited labour adjustment. We use the idea to motivate why recent hiring history may contain information about subsequent adjustment, not to estimate a structural inaction band. The state measure by itself cannot reveal why the firm had been hiring little or whether private equity ownership changed the particular friction responsible for that condition.
A simpler explanation is mean reversion. A firm may have temporarily stopped recruiting, recently contracted, or experienced an unusually quiet hiring period and then resumed normal activity around the transaction date. This possibility is especially important because a low-activity state mechanically creates more scope for subsequent hiring growth. A positive relationship between low prior hiring and later hiring is therefore not informative by itself. Untreated firms with similar hiring histories may show the same pattern.
The empirical design consequently separates two questions. The first is whether low-hiring firms tend to rebound even in the absence of private equity investment. We address that question with untreated pseudo-events processed through the same state construction and estimation procedure as the private equity targets. The second is whether the response following private equity investment is larger than this state-dependent untreated movement. This is why the relevant benchmark for the state gradient is an empirical distribution generated from untreated firms rather than a null of zero.
Temporary inactivity and a broader recent hiring condition also imply different empirical patterns. If the result reflects only a short-lived pause around the transaction, the post-deal response should be closely related to recent employment contraction or to the length of the hiring pause already in progress when the deal closes. A broader pre-deal condition instead predicts a closer relationship with hiring activity measured over the preceding state window and a weaker relationship when the same state is measured much further in the past. Untreated firms in comparable states provide an additional benchmark because they should display the same rebound if private equity investment is unrelated to the subsequent difference.
These comparisons can distinguish some interpretations of the hiring state, but they cannot identify the organisational mechanism producing the post-investment response. Financing, governance, managerial capacity, product demand, and recruiting access remain possible channels. Nor can the observed state eliminate selection on information known to the sponsor but unavailable in our data. The empirical contribution is therefore narrower: we ask whether a target’s recent labour-adjustment condition identifies where post-investment hiring changes are concentrated, whether that pattern appears in gross worker flows or net employment growth, and whether it remains unusually large relative to untreated firms entering the event from comparable observable states.
3. Institutional setting and data
3.1 National Pension records and employment measurement
We use monthly workplace records from Korea's National Pension Scheme (NPS). Covered workplaces report entries and exits of insured workers, insured employment, and the contribution base used to calculate pension contributions. The contribution base is bounded by statutory minimum and maximum values, so measured earnings are top-coded for workers above the applicable ceiling.
The monthly reporting frequency is central to the empirical design. It allows us to distinguish the target’s hiring condition well before the transaction from hiring immediately before and after it. The pre-deal state is measured over months −24 to −13, while the baseline component of the main outcome uses months −12 to −1. The state and outcome windows can therefore be separated without relying on annual observations that would combine activity from different parts of the pre-deal period. Monthly records also place worker entries, exits, and employment on the same time scale, which lets us compare changes in hiring with changes in worker churn and net employment. This distinction is important because a firm can experience substantial gross worker movement without a correspondingly large change in its employment stock.
The reporting structure also determines the unit and coverage of the analysis. NPS enrolment is administered at the workplace (saeopjang) level, whereas we link private equity transactions using the business registration number reported with the pension record. A firm operating through several registered workplaces may therefore require more than one administrative record to represent its employment activity. We construct the panel at the business-registration-number level and separately examine changes in the number of registered workplaces around private equity transactions. The records cover insured workers rather than all individuals who supply labour to the firm; self-employed workers and workers outside NPS coverage are not captured. We require at least five insured employees to reduce measurement problems among the smallest workplaces.
Throughout the paper, hires refers to monthly NPS entries and should be interpreted as an administrative worker inflow. An entry records the acquisition of NPS-insured status at the workplace and may occasionally arise from payroll consolidation, employee re-registration, or another administrative change rather than an external hire. This distinction matters particularly around ownership changes, when administrative reorganisation could otherwise be mistaken for labour-market adjustment. Section 5.2 and Online Appendix C assess this possibility using the employment-flow identity, changes in registered workplaces, and a hiring measure reconstructed without the reported entry count. The results support the use of NPS entries as worker inflows, while the interpretation remains tied to administrative enrolment rather than to an independently observed recruiting decision.
The NPS data therefore provide unusually high-frequency information on labour adjustment but do not observe every margin relevant to employment. We observe when insured workers enter and leave a registered workplace and how the insured employment stock changes; we do not observe job titles, recruiting expenditures, hours worked, informal employment, or the organisational decision that generated a particular entry or exit. The data are consequently well suited to locating the response in worker inflows, exits, and net employment, but not to identifying the managerial or organisational mechanism behind those flows.
3.2 Private equity sample and supplementary data
We identify private equity investments in Korean companies from PitchBook and link them to the pension records using business registration numbers. Additional links recovered from firm names are manually reviewed. The treatment set includes minority growth investments as well as transactions involving a change in control. We therefore use private equity investment as the general term for treated events and reserve buyout or control transaction for the corresponding subsets.
The Korean sample differs from the public-to-private buyouts that feature prominently in much of the U.S. evidence. The targets are predominantly smaller private firms, with median insured employment of 79 workers at the transaction. The sample also includes both domestic and foreign sponsors and contains minority investments alongside control transactions. These features broaden the types of private equity transactions represented in the analysis, but they also affect the scope of the estimates. The results should not be interpreted as estimates for large public-to-private buyouts or for the full population of private equity transactions outside this setting.
The pension panel runs monthly from November 2015 through May 2026. Baseline matched events span December 2016 through June 2026. Because matching variables are measured before the transaction, an event near the end of the panel may enter the matching universe even when it cannot contribute to the corresponding post-deal outcome estimate. The primary gradient sample spans November 2017 through May 2025 because it requires a complete state window and a complete twelve-month post-deal outcome window.
Starting from 752 identified private equity investment events, 168 cannot be linked to a pension record, 120 lack the required pre-deal window, 75 involve fewer than five insured employees at the transaction month, and 10 have no eligible control cell. These restrictions leave 379 events in the baseline matched design. The average-effect analysis requires sufficient observations in both the pre- and post-deal windows for the target and at least one matched control, leaving 367 events.
The primary state-gradient analysis imposes stricter requirements because the target’s hiring state must also be observed and balanced in the matching design. Fifty-three of the 379 matched events lack a complete state window, 17 have no untreated firm in the state-balanced matching cell, 22 lack a usable outcome window, and one falls below the five-employee threshold. The resulting primary sample contains 286 events. The comparison design that matches on industry, size, pre-deal employment growth, and age but not on the hiring state contains 301 events; Section 5.2 compares the two procedures on their common 286-event sample. Analyses of transaction characteristics use between 180 and 301 events depending on data availability, with 180 events observed on all deal characteristics used in the joint comparison. All control pools exclude the full set of 752 identified private equity targets.

Targets entering the matched design have median insured employment of 79.8 workers, compared with 135.6 among identified targets that do not enter it. The matching and outcome requirements therefore tilt the estimation sample toward the smaller end of the observed target population. This selection does not invalidate comparisons within the matched population, but it limits the population to which the estimated gradients should be transported. Our estimates are most informative about private equity investments in smaller Korean private firms for which comparable untreated firms and sufficiently complete pension histories are observed.
Annual audited financial statements provide assets, revenue, cash, debt, interest expense, and operating income. Because statutory criteria determine audit coverage, these statements are available for a different and generally larger subset of firms than the pension-based sample. We use the financial data for supplementary analyses of pre-deal financial condition and post-deal firm outcomes rather than as a requirement for inclusion in the primary hiring analysis.
Annual shareholder records provide holder identity and ownership stakes at annual reference dates. We use these records to confirm the year in which a sponsor stake first appears and to construct ownership measures. Their annual frequency does not identify the transaction month and is therefore less suited than the monthly pension data to locating labour adjustment around the investment date. The transaction date used in the primary event-time analysis comes from the private equity transaction data rather than being inferred from the annual shareholder register.
Taken together, the data combine three types of information at different frequencies: monthly administrative records on insured worker flows and employment, transaction-level information on private equity investments, and annual financial and ownership records for selected subsets of firms. The monthly pension data define the primary hiring-state and worker-flow analyses; transaction and financial variables are used to characterise deals, examine alternative explanations, and study outcomes that are unavailable in the pension records.
4. Research design
4.1 Matching and the pre-deal hiring state
For the average-effect analysis, we match each private equity target to five nearest untreated firms observed in the same deal month. Potential controls must first fall in an exact cell defined by two-digit industry and bins for firm size, pre-deal employment growth, and firm age. A target without an eligible untreated firm in its cell is excluded rather than matched outside the region of common support. This baseline design contains 379 events. Across the characteristics reported in Table 1, Panel D, the largest absolute normalised difference falls from 0.786 in the unmatched comparison pool to 0.095 after matching.
Within each exact cell, the five nearest neighbours are selected using a scaled Euclidean distance based on log pre-deal employment and pre-deal employment growth. The corresponding scale factors are 0.9 and 0.35, and growth is clipped to [−1, 2]. Size is mean insured employment over months −6 to −1, binned at 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 250 employees. Growth is employment in months −6 to −1 relative to months −18 to −13, binned at −10 and +10 percent. Age is years since the firm first enrolled in the pension scheme, binned at 5 and 15 years.
The matching procedure is intended to restrict comparisons to firms that enter the event with similar observable characteristics, not to make selection into private equity investment conditionally random. More generally, matching designs rely on overlap and pre-treatment comparability before outcomes are compared (Stuart 2010). Our use of coarsened covariate cells follows the same logic as exact matching on substantively meaningful ranges of pre-treatment characteristics (Iacus et al. 2012), while the fixed number of nearest neighbours within those cells provides a local comparison among eligible controls (Abadie and Imbens 2006). These references motivate the design logic rather than establish the identifying assumptions in our setting; unobserved differences between targets and controls remain possible.

Balancing the moderator is important because the paper studies a slope across target states, not only an average treated-control difference. Suppose two otherwise similar targets differ in their pre-deal hiring state. If low-hiring untreated firms tend to rebound more than high-hiring untreated firms, controls selected without regard to that state will have different expected outcome paths across the two targets. A regression of the treated-minus-control response on the treated firm’s state can then load on two sources of variation: heterogeneity in the treated response and heterogeneity in the untreated counterfactual.
This problem is distinct from ordinary mean imbalance. Conventional matching can produce controls that are close to targets in industry, size, growth, and age while still allowing the control outcome itself to covary with the moderator used to define the heterogeneity result. In that case, a slope in the matched treatment-control difference need not be a treated-side slope. Part of it can arise because the counterfactual moves systematically across the same state dimension.
The data show that this concern is consequential here. Without state balancing, subsequent changes among the controls are strongly related to the treated firm’s state. Adding the state to the matching cell removes most of that dependence. Section 5.2 shows the decomposition on the common sample: the treated-side gradient is unchanged across the two matching procedures, while the control-side gradient with respect to the treated state falls sharply. The change in the headline gradient therefore comes from the construction of the counterfactual rather than from selecting a different pattern among treated firms.
This feature of the design has a broader implication for heterogeneity analyses based on matched comparisons. If a pre-treatment moderator also organises untreated outcome dynamics, balancing conventional confounders without balancing the moderator can leave the comparison path systematically related to the dimension along which treatment responses are compared. The appropriate response is not necessarily to match exactly on every moderator in every application. Rather, researchers should establish whether the proposed moderator also predicts untreated paths and, when it does, construct the counterfactual so that this relationship does not mechanically enter the estimated heterogeneity.
4.2 Outcome and estimation
The main outcome compares hiring over the twelve months before and after the transaction. For each firm, we calculate the total number of pension-record entries over months −12 through −1 and scale it by mean insured employment over the same period. We construct the corresponding hiring rate for months +1 through +12 and take the change in its logarithm. The target outcome is then differenced against the mean change among its matched controls. The hiring rate is not annualised.
Both twelve-month windows must be fully observed. A firm-window is excluded if any month is missing, mean insured employment is below five, or no hiring is recorded during the window. The last restriction removes three treated firm-windows. The primary transformation is the log of the hiring rate rather than .
The log specification puts proportional changes in hiring on a comparable scale across firms that begin with different hiring rates. A level change of the same proportional magnitude would otherwise be mechanically larger at a firm with a higher initial hiring rate. This choice does not remove all measurement concerns associated with hiring rates: employment appears in the denominator and is itself an outcome. Section 5.2 therefore reports specifications using raw hire counts and conditioning on employment changes, while Section 7.1 addresses the separate mechanical relationship between hiring volume and monthly measures of hiring participation.
The primary estimand is the slope of the matched hiring response with respect to the target’s pre-deal hiring state. It measures how the treated-minus-control change in the log hiring rate varies as prior hiring intensity declines within the matched population. This is not the average effect of private equity investment. Section 5.1 reports that separate quantity. The distinction matters because a positive average response can coexist with substantial variation in its magnitude across target states.
The gradient also has a different interpretation from a forecasting regression. The state is used to organise variation in an observed matched response, conditional on pre-treatment covariates; it is not evaluated here as a model for predicting responses in new targets. Section 7.2 reports an explicit out-of-sample comparison and does not establish a forecasting advantage for the state. The primary estimand should therefore be read as a within-sample heterogeneity parameter for the matched population.
We estimate the gradient after partialling out log pre-deal employment, pre-deal employment growth, firm age, and one-digit industry from both the outcome and the state. All adjustment variables are measured before the transaction. Residualising both sides preserves the interpretation of the coefficient as variation in the matched response associated with the component of the state not accounted for by these observed target characteristics.
The primary specification winsorises the outcome at the 5th and 95th percentiles of the treated sample. The same absolute cut-offs are applied to placebo observations rather than being recalculated within each placebo sample. This keeps the transformation fixed when the treated estimate is compared with the empirical null. Alternative transformations, winsorisation choices, and selected matching specifications are reported in Online Appendix B.
4.3 Inference
Inference for the state gradient must accommodate a feature that makes a conventional zero-centred reference distribution unattractive in this setting. Firms with unusually low prior hiring can increase hiring subsequently even when no private equity investment occurs. Mean reversion, temporary pauses, and other state-dependent untreated dynamics can therefore generate a positive relationship between low prior activity and subsequent hiring.
We estimate that background relationship directly from untreated firms. Each matched control firm is treated in turn as a pseudo-event, matched to five controls using the same procedure as the private equity targets, and passed through the same state construction, adjustment, estimation, and winsorisation steps. This produces 1,246 untreated pseudo-events. We draw samples from this pool at the size of the treated sample 2,000 times to obtain the empirical reference distribution of the state gradient.
The resulting distribution is not assumed to be centred at zero. It answers a more demanding question: how large a state gradient arises when firms with no private equity event are subjected to the same procedure? State-dependent rebound, functional-form effects, and features of the matching and estimation pipeline that also operate among untreated firms can therefore contribute to the benchmark. The primary estimate is evaluated against this distribution rather than against a hypothetical absence of untreated rebound.
The pseudo-event exercise should not be interpreted as a randomisation test. Private equity investment is not randomly assigned, and the untreated pseudo-events do not reproduce an experimental assignment mechanism. The exercise instead provides an empirical null for the particular pattern studied in the paper: the relationship between prior hiring state and subsequent matched hiring changes when the focal event is absent. It addresses whether ordinary untreated dynamics can reproduce the observed state gradient, but it cannot remove bias from unobserved information that systematically distinguishes actual targets from otherwise similar untreated firms.
The primary hiring-state prediction is directional, so hiring-gradient tests use the upper tail of the placebo distribution. Alternative specifications of the same hiring gradient use the same upper-tail test. Employment, worker churn, separations, and pre-deal gradients have no assigned directional prediction and are evaluated using two-sided placebo tests.
Confidence intervals are obtained by resampling matched target-control groups so that each target and its controls move together. For the primary gradient, we report the wider interval obtained when uncertainty is clustered on treated firms. The 286 target events use 1,144 distinct control firms; 95 percent of those controls appear in only one event and none in more than three. Online Appendix D reports alternative treatment of this limited control reuse. Because clustering on control firms yields a narrower interval, the treated-firm-clustered interval is retained as the primary, more conservative interval.
We also distinguish an imprecise estimate from evidence that an effect is economically small. An interval containing zero is described as not detected unless the entire interval lies within a stated range of practical equivalence. This distinction is particularly important for secondary outcomes such as separations and monthly allocation measures, where failure to reject zero does not by itself establish the absence of an economically meaningful change.
The pre-deal hiring-state gradient in the log hiring rate is the primary analysis, with the average post-deal effects serving as a benchmark. Monthly hiring patterns, transaction and sponsor characteristics, and worker earnings and value added are secondary analyses. Alternative state definitions and windows and the alternative treatment-timing designs are treated as exploratory and reported in the Online Appendix.
A causal interpretation requires more than balance on observed covariates. Conditional on the matching variables, the pre-deal hiring state, and the pre-treatment variables used in estimation, targets and matched controls must provide comparable state-specific counterfactual hiring changes in the absence of private equity investment. The state-balanced design addresses one observable violation of this requirement: counterfactual outcomes that vary systematically with the moderator. It does not address selection on information that is observed by the sponsor but absent from our data. A sponsor may invest in a low-hiring target because it anticipates future demand, a new contract, financing, managerial change, or another expansion opportunity that comparable untreated firms do not share. Section 6 examines the observable implications of this class of explanations and the extent to which pre-deal dynamics constrain them, but sponsor private information remains a limitation of the design.
5. Main results
5.1 Average post-investment responses
Private equity targets increase both employment and hiring relative to their matched controls. Twelve months after the transaction, employment is 0.0878 log points higher [0.0462, 0.1300], or approximately 9 percent. Over the same pre- to post-deal comparison, the twelve-month hiring rate increases by 0.0485 [0.0163, 0.0814], equivalent to 4.85 percentage points. These estimates use the 367 events with sufficient observations on both sides of the transaction (Table 2).
The corresponding change in the separation rate is 0.0007 [−0.0353, 0.0398]. The interval excludes a separation change as large in absolute magnitude as the increase in the hiring rate, while remaining compatible with smaller movements in either direction. A supplementary monthly specification gives a hiring hazard ratio of 1.159 [1.083, 1.241].
Monthly hiring participation and hiring intensity within active months both rise after the transaction. Because both measures respond mechanically to the total volume of hiring, we treat this decomposition as descriptive rather than as evidence of a separate adjustment margin. Section 7.1 compares the monthly pattern with a benchmark that holds realised hiring volume fixed.

5.2 Hiring responses by pre-deal hiring intensity
The average effect conceals substantial variation across targets. Firms entering the transaction with lower hiring intensity subsequently increase hiring much more than firms that had already been hiring actively. Under the primary specification, the gradient in the treated-minus-control change in the log hiring rate is 0.7101, with a treated-firm-clustered 95 percent bootstrap interval of [0.3187, 1.1254] (Table 3, Panel A; Figure 1). An interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.2005-log-point larger response [0.0900, 0.3177], or approximately 22 percent on the corresponding rate scale.
Low-hiring untreated firms also rebound to some extent. The placebo distribution generated from 1,246 untreated pseudo-events has a mean of 0.101 and a standard deviation of 0.1538. The observed gradient exceeds this mean by 0.6091 log points and lies 3.96 standard deviations above it (upper-tail placebo ). The relevant benchmark is therefore the state-dependent movement among untreated firms rather than zero.

When hiring activity is measured two to three years before the transaction, using months −36 to −25, the gradient falls to 0.268, close to its specification-specific placebo mean of 0.141. Recent hiring history carries considerably more information about the response than the same measure taken further in the past.
The pattern is also more closely related to the firm’s broader recent hiring history than to a short pause immediately before the transaction. Adding pre-deal employment growth leaves the state coefficient at 0.410 [0.078, 0.753]. The gradient on the historical share of no-hire months is 0.559 [0.241, 0.885], compared with 0.040 for the length of the hiring pause already in progress at closing. When the two measures enter together, the corresponding coefficients are 0.536 and 0.032. At the same time, size, industry, growth, age, and seasonality jointly account for 63.9 percent of the variation in the state, and the residualised-state estimate is positive but imprecise. These results favour a relatively recent hiring condition over either a temporary pause at closing or a fixed firm type.
The administrative entry records also behave largely as worker inflows. Across the monthly panel, the median accounting discrepancy is 1.4 percent of employment, and changes in the number of registered workplaces around transactions are rare. Reconstructing hires from employment changes and recorded separations yields a positive gradient of 0.365 (upper-tail placebo ). Filters that remove months with larger accounting discrepancies retain positive point estimates but reduce precision. These checks support the use of pension entries as worker inflows while leaving the measurement qualification in Section 3.1 intact; Online Appendix C reports the full set of checks.
The result is not driven by employment in the denominator. Replacing the hiring rate with the raw log count of hires gives a gradient of 0.6315, with a placebo mean of 0.1795. Adding the change in log employment as a covariate gives a gradient of 0.6957, compared with a placebo mean of 0.0363. The corresponding gradient in log employment is −0.0580. Most of the 0.7101 log-rate gradient therefore reflects differences in worker entries themselves.
5.3 Gross worker-flow adjustment
Net employment varies differently with the pre-deal hiring state. The gradient in log employment is −0.0580, compared with a placebo mean of 0.0796 (two-sided ). Firms with the largest hiring responses thus have smaller employment responses along the same state dimension. This cross-sectional result does not conflict with the average effect in Section 5.1: employment still rises by approximately 9 percent on average across matched targets.
Worker churn, by contrast, moves in the same direction as hiring. Its state gradient is 0.3816, with a standardised distance of 3.08 from the placebo benchmark. Within-event contrasts make the difference with employment particularly clear. The hiring-minus-employment gradient is 0.7536, compared with a placebo mean of 0.0232, and the churn-minus-employment gradient is 0.4539 (Table 4). The stronger response among low-hiring targets is concentrated in worker inflows and gross worker-flow activity rather than in larger net employment gains.

6. Validity and alternative explanations
The state-balanced design addresses observable differences in the counterfactual, but it does not by itself establish that private equity investment causes the larger hiring response among low-hiring targets. Three concerns are particularly relevant. First, firms with unusually low hiring may rebound even without a transaction. Second, private equity targets may already be moving onto different hiring paths before the investment. Third, sponsors may select firms using information about future expansion that is not observed in our data. The empirical design provides different amounts of leverage over these three possibilities.
The first concern is built directly into the untreated pseudo-event benchmark. Low-hiring control firms are allowed to experience their own subsequent recovery, and the empirical null is therefore not centred at zero. As reported in Section 5.2, untreated pseudo-events produce a mean state gradient of 0.101, compared with 0.7101 among private equity targets. This comparison rules out the simplest version of mechanical mean reversion in which low-hiring firms generally rebound by an amount comparable to the target response. It does not, however, address a factor that is specific to firms subsequently selected for private equity investment.
Pre-deal outcomes provide evidence on whether such a factor was already visible before the transaction. The primary comparison avoids overlap between the hiring-state and outcome windows. Because the main state is measured over months −24 to −13, we move the state back to months −36 to −25 when constructing the earlier comparison. Relative to the corresponding untreated pseudo-event benchmark, the pre-deal excess gradient is −0.0777 [−0.4394, 0.0854] across 250 events. The interval excludes a positive pre-deal divergence comparable in magnitude to the post-deal gradient of 0.7101. It remains compatible with smaller pre-existing differences, and the lower end of the interval also permits sizeable movements in the opposite direction.
Using the usual state window gives a similar ordering but is less clean because the state and earlier outcome share observations. Among the 283 events available for this comparison, the pre-deal gradient is 0.1068 [−0.1955, 0.4351], whereas the post-deal gradient on the same events is 0.7250 [0.3259, 1.1081]. The comparison does not show a pre-deal gradient approaching the magnitude of the subsequent response, although its precision is not sufficient to treat the pre-period difference as exactly zero.
A relative-magnitude sensitivity calculation following Rambachan and Roth (2023) provides a complementary way to express this uncertainty. The breakdown value is 3.126: the post-deal estimate remains distinguishable from zero when the permitted differential trend is allowed to reach 3.126 times the largest observed pre-deal gradient. The calculation asks how large departures from the observed pre-deal pattern would have to become before the post-deal conclusion changes. It is a sensitivity calibration, not a test that establishes the identifying assumption or converts the matched comparison into a causal design.

These comparisons restrict two relatively observable explanations for the result. The untreated pseudo-events show that ordinary state-dependent recovery is much smaller than the gradient among private equity targets. The pre-deal analysis, in turn, does not reveal a positive divergence of comparable magnitude before the transaction. Together, they weigh against an account in which the main result is simply the continuation of hiring dynamics that are already visible either among untreated low-hiring firms or among the eventual targets themselves.
They provide much less leverage against selection on sponsor private information. A sponsor may know that demand is about to increase, that a major customer or contract is likely to materialise, that financing for a planned expansion has become available, or that a management change will be followed by additional hiring. Such information can matter even when the firm’s observed employment and hiring path remains flat before the transaction. If sponsors disproportionately invest in low-hiring firms precisely when they expect these changes, matched untreated firms with similar observed histories need not represent the relevant counterfactual.
The timing of this information matters for what the pre-deal tests can detect. Information that affects realised hiring before the transaction can generate an observable pre-trend and is therefore constrained by Table 5. Information that sponsors possess but do not act on until the investment occurs need not leave such a trace. The absence of a comparable pre-deal gradient consequently narrows the range of selection stories, but it cannot rule out forward-looking selection based on signals that remain latent in the administrative outcomes.
Supplementary analyses provide context for this possibility rather than resolving it. Online Appendix D reports the deployment-pressure comparison, the subsample with independently confirmed transaction dates, alternative treatment of shared controls, and the separate differential-trend sensitivity calculation for the average-effect estimand. The deployment-pressure result is suggestive against the simplest private-information timing account because the hiring response is larger when the sponsor’s most recent fund is older, a setting in which investment timing may be less discretionary. The comparison is not decisive: older-fund deals can differ in target composition and other dimensions, and the proxy does not observe the sponsor’s information set directly.
Alternative treatment-timing designs are still less conclusive. The not-yet-treated comparison is imprecise, only thirteen usable sponsor exits are available, and the exit estimates allow both substantial reversal and persistence. A comparison with non-private-equity ownership changes produces little movement in the relevant labour outcomes and therefore provides a weak benchmark for separating a private equity response from ownership change more generally. These exercises are reported in Online Appendix G because their limited support makes them more informative about what the current sample cannot resolve than about the direction of the main effect.
The evidence therefore supports a bounded interpretation of the state gradient. A response of similar magnitude does not arise from the observed rebound of untreated low-hiring firms, and the non-overlapping pre-deal comparison does not show a positive divergence comparable to the post-deal estimate. These findings reduce the plausibility of observable mean reversion and continuation of an existing hiring trend as complete explanations. They do not eliminate sponsor selection on information about future firm conditions that is absent from the pension, transaction, and financial records. The main estimates are accordingly most informative about the unusually large post-investment hiring response among low-hiring targets relative to observably comparable untreated firms; a causal interpretation remains conditional on the absence of sufficiently strong unobserved selection.
7. Additional results
7.1 Post-deal dynamics and monthly hiring patterns
The increase in hiring begins soon after the private equity investment and persists beyond the first year. Figure 1(a) shows that the quarterly treated-minus-control hiring rate rises in the first post-deal quarter, with a 95 percent interval of [0.0032, 0.0250], and remains positive through the tenth quarter. The first four post-deal quarters sum to 0.048, closely matching the 4.85-percentage-point twelve-month increase reported in Table 2.
Targets also hire in a broader set of months after the transaction. This pattern is mechanically related to total hiring volume: a firm that hires more workers over a fixed period will generally record positive hiring in more months even if the timing of those hires is otherwise unchanged.
To separate this mechanical component from changes in monthly allocation, we construct a firm-specific volume benchmark. Within each firm-window, realised total hires are held fixed and reallocated across the same observed months in proportion to monthly employment. The benchmark therefore preserves total hiring, the number of observed months, and monthly employment exposure.
The observed share of months with no hiring falls by 0.0466 [−0.0675, −0.0270]. The corresponding volume benchmark predicts a decline of 0.0518 [−0.0699, −0.0326]. The excess change is 0.0052 [−0.0082, 0.0189], with the entire interval inside the ±0.046 practical-equivalence range used to assess whether a residual timing component could be as large as the observed change itself (Table 6).

7.2 Transaction and sponsor characteristics
Observed transaction characteristics explain relatively little of the variation in hiring responses in the common sample. Before adjustment, the difference between control transactions and growth investments is 0.1218 log points [−0.0494, 0.2776]. Targets in control transactions also enter the deal with lower hiring activity. Adjusting for the pre-deal hiring state, firm size, prior employment growth, age, industry, and deal year reduces the estimated deal-type difference to 0.0475 [−0.1062, 0.1958]. Part of the unadjusted deal-type difference therefore reflects differences in the condition of the firms entering those transactions. Additional transaction- and sponsor-level comparisons are reported in Online Appendix E.
Among the 180 events with complete deal information, control transfer, acquired stake, and sponsor deal count jointly explain 0.92 percent of event-level response variation, with a permutation . The pre-deal hiring state alone explains 2.86 percent on the same observations, with a permutation . Within this sample, the target’s prior hiring condition therefore has greater explanatory content than the observed transaction characteristics.
This difference does not carry over clearly to held-out observations. Under five-fold cross-fitting, out-of-sample  is −0.0269 for the deal-characteristic model and −0.0168 for the hiring state. Their difference is 0.0101 [−0.0349, 0.1173]. 7.3 Worker earnings and value added
Employment growth after the transaction is accompanied by a modest increase in measured earnings per worker. Average assessed income rises by 0.0096 log points relative to matched controls [0.0039, 0.0154] across 346 events. Because assessed income is the contribution base recorded in the National Pension data and is subject to statutory top-coding, it measures pensionable earnings rather than total compensation.
Across 182 events, the treated-control difference in the implied wage of marginal hires is 0.0086 [−0.1383, 0.1585]. The interval excludes a 20 percent discount relative to incumbent earnings, although smaller differences remain compatible with the data. Because wage growth among incumbent workers also affects this construction, we interpret only the treated-control difference; Online Appendix F provides additional details on the construction.
Value added also increases among firms with audited financial statements. One year after the transaction, value added is 0.0778 log points higher relative to matched controls [0.0296, 0.1294] across 147 events. Value added per worker changes by −0.0052 log points [−0.0333, 0.0261]. The full interval lies within the ±0.10-log-point practical-equivalence range. Employment and value added therefore grow at broadly similar rates in this audited subsample.
These results provide context for the hiring and employment estimates but do not identify a mechanism. Measured pensionable earnings rise modestly, and audited firms expand value added alongside employment. The estimates do not identify the marginal productivity or profitability of additional workers, nor do they distinguish among financing, demand, governance, and other organisational channels.
The financial results come from a more selected sample than the primary hiring analysis. The value-added estimates use 147 events, compared with 286 in the primary state-gradient sample, and audited firms are generally larger. These estimates should therefore be read as supporting evidence for post-deal expansion among audited targets rather than as results that automatically generalise to the full matched sample.
8. Conclusion
Private equity targets in Korea show markedly different post-investment hiring responses depending on their hiring activity before the transaction. Firms entering the deal with lower hiring intensity subsequently increase hiring more strongly than firms that had already been hiring actively. An interquartile movement toward lower pre-deal hiring intensity corresponds to a 0.20-log-point larger response. Untreated firms in comparable states also rebound to some extent, but the gradient among private equity targets lies well above the distribution generated by untreated pseudo-events processed through the same matching and estimation procedure. Post-investment hiring adjustment is thus concentrated among targets that enter the transaction from a low-hiring state.
The same state dimension produces a different pattern in net employment. Low-hiring targets experience larger increases in hiring and churn, while their employment response is smaller. The cross-target difference is therefore concentrated in gross worker-flow activity rather than in larger net employment gains. The separation gradient is positive but too imprecise to isolate worker exits as a separate margin, so the results do not identify a specific reallocation mechanism.
This evidence adds the target’s pre-deal labour-adjustment condition to the dimensions used to understand heterogeneity in private equity employment outcomes. Existing work has emphasised transaction type, financing conditions, and sponsor characteristics. In the matched Korean sample, pre-deal hiring intensity has greater within-sample explanatory content than the observed transaction and sponsor characteristics, although the out-of-sample comparison does not establish it as a forecasting variable. The contribution is therefore about the incidence of post-investment adjustment within the observed sample, not about a general rule for predicting which targets will respond.
The matching results also have a design implication. When a pre-treatment characteristic is used to study heterogeneous responses and the same characteristic predicts untreated outcome dynamics, balancing only conventional covariates can leave the comparison group systematically related to the moderator. In our setting, adding the pre-deal hiring state to the matching design removes most of that dependence while leaving the treated-side gradient unchanged. More generally, the moderator should enter the construction of the counterfactual when it also organises untreated outcome paths.
The remaining limitation is selection on information that the researcher does not observe. Financing, governance, managerial capacity, demand, and recruiting access remain possible internal channels, and sponsors may know about future firm conditions before the transaction. The pre-deal and untreated-firm evidence weighs against a simple observable-rebound explanation but cannot eliminate selection on sponsor private information. The estimates also apply to the smaller private firms represented in the matched Korean sample. Subject to these limits, the central result is that recent hiring history identifies where post-investment workforce adjustment is most pronounced, and that the adjustment appears primarily in gross worker flows rather than differential net employment growth.
