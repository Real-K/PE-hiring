Appendix A. Hiring-volume benchmark and monthly measures
The main text shows that private equity targets hire in a broader set of months after investment. Some of this change follows mechanically from the increase in total hiring: when a firm hires more workers over a fixed period, positive hiring will generally be observed in more months even if the allocation of hires across months is otherwise unchanged. This appendix compares the observed monthly pattern with a benchmark that holds realised hiring volume fixed. It also reports the extensive- and intensive-margin decomposition of the average increase in hiring.

The exercise is therefore a benchmark for the mechanical effect of hiring volume on monthly participation and concentration measures, rather than a structural model of hiring timing.
Before the transaction, treated firms average 91.9 hires per twelve-month window. They have 3.23 months without hiring, compared with 2.58 under the volume benchmark, and their longest no-hire spell averages 1.97 months, compared with 1.53 under the benchmark. The Herfindahl index of monthly hires is 0.203 in the data and 0.163 under the benchmark; the corresponding shares of hires in the two busiest months are 0.468 and 0.378. Hiring is therefore more concentrated across months than the volume-preserving allocation would predict even before the transaction.

Appendix B. State construction and alternative specifications
The primary analysis measures the target’s pre-deal hiring state from hiring activity over months −24 to −13 and orients the index so that higher values indicate lower hiring intensity. This appendix examines alternative ways to summarise that history and selected changes to the outcome transformation, state window, matching design, and estimation procedure. Measurement checks for the administrative worker-entry measure are reported separately in Appendix C.
B.1 Alternative measures of the pre-deal hiring state
Pre-deal hiring activity can be summarised by the hiring rate, total hiring volume, or the frequency of months with no hiring. These measures capture related but distinct aspects of the firm’s recent hiring history and need not contain the same information after conditioning on observable firm characteristics.
All measures in Table B1 are constructed over months −24 to −13 and oriented so that a positive coefficient indicates a larger post-deal hiring response among less active firms. The adjusted specifications partial out log pre-deal employment, pre-deal employment growth, firm age, and one-digit industry from both the state and the outcome. Appendix C. Measurement of worker entries and flow outcomes
The main analysis uses monthly National Pension entries as a measure of worker inflows. Two features of the administrative records require additional scrutiny. An entry need not always correspond to an external hire: payroll consolidation, employee re-registration, or other administrative changes around a transaction can generate entries without new recruitment. In addition, the hiring and separation rates scale worker flows by employment, which can itself change after investment. This appendix examines the accounting consistency of entries, exits, and employment; reconstructs hiring without using the reported entry field; and reports additional diagnostics on worker flows and employment.
C.1 Accounting consistency of worker entries and exits
The pension records report monthly insured employment, worker entries, and worker exits. These quantities should approximately satisfy the employment-flow identity: changes in insured employment should correspond to entries minus exits, apart from differences arising from administrative timing or reporting.
Across 227,339 firm-month observations, the records reconcile closely on average. The median absolute discrepancy is one worker, and 24.3 percent of firm-months reconcile exactly. Relative to employment, the median absolute discrepancy is 1.4 percent and the 90th percentile is 7.1 percent.

C.3 Hiring rates, employment denominators, and level flows
The primary hiring outcome scales worker entries by employment. Because employment can also change after private equity investment, variation in the denominator can contribute to variation in the hiring rate. The main text addresses this issue using the raw log count of hires and a specification that conditions on the change in log employment.
The gradient in the raw log count of hires is 0.6315, compared with a placebo mean of 0.1795. Conditioning on the change in log employment gives a gradient of 0.6957, compared with a placebo mean of 0.0363. The corresponding gradient in log employment is −0.0580 against a placebo mean of 0.0796. The large positive hiring-rate gradient therefore remains visible when worker entries are analysed without employment in the denominator.
Level flow rates provide a separate accounting comparison. The state gradients in the hiring rate, separation rate, and net inflow rate measured in levels are +0.263, +0.094, and +0.172, respectively. The relation between the three estimated gradients satisfies the corresponding flow identity to within 0.003.
The positive gradient in the level net-inflow rate and the negative gradient in log employment refer to different objects. The level net-inflow rate divides net worker flows by employment, whereas log employment measures the employment stock directly. Changes in the employment denominator can therefore affect the former even when the latter moves in the opposite direction. We use log employment as the main measure of differential net employment growth.

C.5 Joint worker-flow pattern
Hiring, churn, and employment are correlated outcomes, so their joint configuration contains information that is not captured by separate univariate comparisons alone. We summarise the joint deviation from the untreated placebo distribution using the Mahalanobis distance, which scales the difference between the observed gradient vector and the placebo mean by the covariance matrix of the placebo gradients.
The observed vector of hiring, churn, and employment gradients has a Mahalanobis distance of 28.4 from the placebo mean. The 95th percentile of the placebo distribution is 7.6. The hiring and churn gradients have a correlation of approximately 0.71 under the placebo distribution, making the covariance adjustment consequential for the joint comparison.
The observed combination is characterised by a large positive hiring gradient, a positive churn gradient, and a negative employment gradient. Relative to the joint untreated distribution, this vector lies well outside the range generated by most pseudo-events. The multivariate result provides an additional summary of the gross-flow pattern; it does not identify the contribution of separations or distinguish among the organisational mechanisms that could generate the worker-flow response.

The untreated quarterly gradients are also variable. Two of the eight untreated pre- and post-deal quarterly estimates are individually different from zero. This variation further limits what can be learned from quarter-by-quarter comparisons in the present sample.
D.2 Sponsor deployment pressure
Sponsor private information remains a limitation of the matched design. One possible form of such selection is transaction timing: sponsors with greater discretion may wait to invest until favourable expansion prospects become apparent to them but remain unobserved by the researcher.
We examine this possibility using the age of the sponsor’s most recent private equity fund at the deal date as a proxy for deployment pressure. Fund age is measured from the most recent fund close month; when the close date is unavailable, we use the midpoint of the vintage year. High deployment pressure is defined as the top tercile of fund age, with a cutoff of 2.42 years.
In a discrete-time complementary log-log model, the treated-by-post-by-pressure interaction has a hazard ratio of 1.2881 [1.0535, 1.5750]. The model is estimated on 379 event clusters, 4,686 grouped cells, and 48,853 firm-month observations and includes event fixed effects and duration-bucket fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by event.
Under this proxy, the post-deal hiring response is larger among transactions associated with older sponsor funds. This pattern is not consistent with a narrow timing-selection account in which sponsors with greater flexibility wait for especially favourable expansion opportunities. The comparison does not identify exogenous variation in sponsor timing, however. Fund age may also be related to sponsor strategy, target composition, fundraising conditions, or other unobserved characteristics of the transaction. The result therefore does not remove selection on sponsor private information.
D.3 Independent confirmation of transaction timing
PitchBook provides the transaction dates used in the main analysis. Annual shareholder records offer an independent timing check by identifying the year in which the sponsor stake first appears.
The deal year can be independently corroborated for 62 events. In this subsample, the treated-minus-control change in the share of no-hire months is −0.0645 [−0.1145, −0.0163], compared with approximately −0.047 in the full sample.
Because the confirmed-date sample is small and the outcome is the no-hire share rather than the primary log hiring-rate gradient, this comparison is informative mainly about transaction-date measurement. The direction of the result is similar in the confirmed subsample, making moderate deal-year miscoding an unlikely explanation for the observed post-deal hiring pattern.
The shareholder records are annual, however, and therefore corroborate the deal year rather than the exact transaction month. The exercise does not address within-year timing error.

D.5 Relative-magnitude sensitivity of the average effect
The average post-deal effect and the hiring-state gradient are distinct estimands, so their sensitivity calculations should be interpreted separately. For the average-effect estimand, the relative-magnitude framework of Rambachan and Roth (2023) yields a breakdown value of 0.658.
The breakdown value is the relative magnitude of permitted post-treatment differential trends at which the confidence set first includes zero. A value of 0.658 therefore means that the average-effect estimate becomes indistinguishable from zero before the permitted post-treatment deviation reaches the size of the largest observed pre-treatment quarterly movement.
The corresponding breakdown value for the primary state gradient is 3.126 at the twelve-month resolution used in the main text. The two values should not be compared as estimates of a common parameter: they apply to different estimands, outcome constructions, and temporal resolutions. They do show that the average-effect estimate is more sensitive to departures from the corresponding trend restriction than the primary state-gradient estimate.

F.3 Sample coverage and longer-horizon survival
The audited financial-statement sample is smaller and more selected than the primary hiring sample. The one-year value-added analysis contains 147 events, compared with 286 events in the primary state-gradient analysis, and audited firms are generally larger. We do not reweight the financial-statement subsample to match the full primary sample. The accounting results therefore describe the audited targets for which the required statements are available.
Longer-horizon survival outcomes require an additional qualification. Leaving the observed panel can reflect closure, acquisition, restructuring, or a successful sale, so disappearance from the pension records does not have a unique economic interpretation.
At the four-year horizon, survival is 0.136 lower among targets with lower pre-deal hiring activity than among those with higher activity [−0.2563, −0.0289]. This horizon has substantial sample attrition: approximately one-third of otherwise usable events are lost near the end of the panel. The corresponding state contrasts are not separately detected at the earlier horizons, and the minimum multiplicity-adjusted -value across the four horizons is 0.074.
The four-year estimate should therefore be interpreted cautiously. It appears at the horizon with the greatest loss of observations, does not reproduce at earlier horizons, and panel exit does not uniquely identify firm failure. We treat it as a longer-horizon diagnostic rather than evidence of a persistent adverse survival effect among low-hiring targets.

Notes. The conventional-matching interaction is estimated on 308 events with the required earlier state measurement, 3,919 grouped cells, and 41,303 firm-month observations. “Conventional matching” refers to the design in which the hiring state is not included in the exact matching cell. The state-balanced continuous-state specification uses 1,199 grouped cells. In the state-balanced tercile specification, the state-tercile indicator is collinear with the event fixed effects and cannot be separately estimated.
The hazard interaction and the primary state gradient are therefore not estimates of the same object using the same source of variation. The primary specification estimates an event-level treated-minus-control response across the target’s continuous pre-deal state after balancing controls on that state. The monthly hazard specification instead conditions on duration and incorporates event fixed effects.
Under the state-balanced design, the continuous hazard interaction remains positive but is estimated imprecisely. The monthly hazard model therefore does not provide a precise independent estimate of state dependence under the primary matching design.
